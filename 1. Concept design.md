At the beginning of a new aircraft project only few specifications and requirements
are given. The first step, called conceptual design, consists in transforming these
few information into a configuration that can meet the initial requirements: it
is a high level operation which defines a general idea of the aircraft, i.e., wings
number and position, tail configuration, engines position and so on. In this step
several configurations (but also elements of a configuration) can be selected and
developed with more details for a final comparison and choice (for example two
tail options are selected and investigated further to identify the best one from a
control point of view). The second step in aircraft design is called preliminary
design; in this phase a set of fundamental parameters are computed: wing surface
S, take-off weight WT O and required power Pb. These values are the cornerstone
of the whole design, from aircraft size to performance and costs.
This chapter presents these two steps for the sizing of a VTOL UAV: initially
the configuration is selected, then fundamental parameters are computed and, to
check the robustness of the obtained solution, a sensitivity analysis is performed.

#### Step 1:-

Take off weight :-

$$W_TO = W_E + W_Fuel + W_PL + W_Crew$$

Where  $$W_Fuel = Fuel weight$$,  $$W_Pl = Payload weight$$,  $$W_E = empty weight$$,  $$W_Crew = Crew weight$$

 $$W_Fuel (W_To)$$ is fuel fraction method based on the mission profile for generic mission profile made of takeoff, climb, cruise, decent and landing.

 $$1-\frac{W_fuel}{W_To} = \frac{W_final}{W_lnd} \frac{W_lnd}{W_decent} \frac{W_decent}{W_cruise} \frac{W_cruise}{W_climb} \frac{W_climb}{W_To}$$

 Each fraction represents the change in weight due to fuel burnt in each flight phase

$$W_To = \frac{W_crew + W_Pl}{1-\frac{W_E}{W_To} - \frac{W_fuel}{W_To}}$$

The result is take-off weight WTo

# 1.3 Sizing setup 

The weight breakdown for eVTOL is

 $$W_To = W_E + W_batt + W_Pl$$

Now the power consumption of each mission profile leg is computed using simple flight mechanics equation.

UAV takeoff and climb phase is the first segment so finding required power to climb $$P_b^cl$$

$$P_b^cl = \frac{T_cl * Vi}{FM}$$

Where $$T_cl$$ is the thrust during climb. 

Vi the induced velocity on the rotors disk and FM the rotor figure of merit.

The induced velocity is 

$$Vi = \frac{ROC}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{ROC}{2}^2 + \frac{T_cl}{2\rho 0 A_prop}}$$

While the required thrust in climb is 

$$T_cl = W_To + \frac{1}{2} \rho 0 ROC^2 Sp CD_cl$$

ROC is Rate of climb 

$$\rho 0 $$ the density at ground level.$$A_prop$$ the overall propeller area ,Sp the horizontal projected area of the UAV and CDcl the drag coefficient in climb.

The power required for climb is assumed equal to the power required for descent.

Now cruise phase are analyzed, Power in cruise is computed starting from the horizontal level flight power equilibrium.

$$P_b^cr = D_ver$$

With D drag and Vcr cruise speed. The substitution of the conventional drag definition 

$$D = \frac{1}{2}pv^2 SCD$$

Therefore,

$$P_b^cr = \frac{1}{2} pcrV^3_cr CD_cr\frac{W_To}{\frac{W_To}{S}FF}$$

Where CDcr is the drag coefficient in cruise phase,Pcr is the density at cruise altitude and $${\frac{W_To}{S}FF}$$ the wing loading for FF obtained from the SMP.

The vtol works as a multirotor in hovering as in climb and descent phase S, So the required power is computed with the momentum theory.

$$P_b^ho = \frac{Tho^\frac{3}{2}}{\sqrt{2\rho 0 A_prop}}$$

With Tho thrust in hovering condition defined as 

Tho = WTo .... Due to vertical equilibrium.

# 1.6 Wing sizing 

The wings only works in forward flight. It does nothing during hover. So wing sizing is a level flight equilibrium problem: lift = wright

Formula:

$$V_{stall} = sqrt\frac{2W}{\rho S C_{L_{max}}}$$

which is then forms;

$$S= \frac{2W}{\rho V^2_{stall} C_{L_{max}}}$$

Where,

1. W = weight in Newtons = m * 9.81
2. $$\rho$$ = air density = 1.225$$kg/m^3$$
3. $$C_{L_{max}}$$ = max lift coefficient of airfoil..... its basically nearly 1.2-1.4 for plain airfoil.
4. $$V_{stall}$$ = the slowest speed the wing can still hold up.

How to choose  $$V_{stall}$$ : Pick your desired cruise speed first consider payload etc. Then  $$V_{stall}$$ =  $$V_{cruise}$$/1.2 to  $$V_{cruise}$$/1.3(a 20-30% margin for flying near stall). 

for eg: 15m/s,  $$V_{stall}$$ = 12m/s.

Example 5kg, $$ C_{L_{max}}$$ = 1.3, $$V_{stall}$$ = 12m/s

$$S= \frac{25 * 9.81}{1.225 *  12^2 * 1.3}$$ = $$\frac{98.1}{229.3} = 0.428m^2$$

Then pick the aspect ratio (AR) to get span

$$AR = \frac{b^2}{S}$$....$$b = \sqrt{AR * S}$$,  ... c = S/b

What we need to consider in AR: Higher is the AR nearly 8-12 = which means more efficient cruise which is also known as lower induced drag, but structure gets heavier and harder to keep rigid without a spar. 

Where as lower AR which is near 5-7 = which is stiffer, lighter and less efficient. For hover where cruise efficiency isnt only concern, AR should be nearly 6-8.

Therefore, AR = 7:b = $$\sqrt{7 * 0.428}$$ = 1.73m, c = 0.428/1.73 = 0.247m which is nearly 25cm chord.

What is chord?

It refers to the imaginary straight line drawn directly from the leading edge to the trailing edge of an airfoil cross-section.

Airfoil pick: At this size and speed, Reynolds number matters

$$Re = \frac{\rho V c}{\eta}$$, $$\eta_{air} = 1.81 * 10^-{5}$$ Pa/cdotps

At V=15, c=0.247: Re = (1.225×15×0.247)/1.81e-5 ≈ 250,000. That's low-Reynolds territory — use airfoils designed for it: Clark Y, NACA 4412, SD7037, or Eppler 205, not a generic NACA00xx symmetric section (poor low-Re performance, low $$C_{L_{max}}$$.

#### What to consider for the pusher motor

Size it for cruise power continuous, but check static thrust separately at zero forward speed the
pusher needs enough thrust to accelerate the plane up to a speed where the wing generates useful
lift. Undersizing static thrust here is a common transition-phase failure mode: the aircraft
"sags" losing altitude during the hover-to-cruise handoff because the pusher can't accelerate it
fast enough while lift motors are already spinning down.

# 1.9 Fuselag or Body Sizing 

Here the most important in weight and CG point of view is Battery and later rest all electronics components.

Here we need to find the CG for this as per weight given above (1.5 point) so we need to initially design models and assemble with their mass respectively and calculate CG.

The primary formula for the longitudinal CG (x-axis) is the mass-weighted average of all component positions:

formula:

$$CG_x = \frac{\sum m_i * x_i}{\sum m_i}$$

Where 
mi is the mass of each component (fuselage, wings, motors, batteries, rotors) and xi is its
distance from a defined datum. For VTOL designs, it is critical to model the lift motor booms and
additional wiring accurately, as these add significant weight that shifts the CG.

## Target CG Location
For the fixed-wing mode of a VTOL, the CG should typically be located at 25–30% of the Mean
Aerodynamic Chord (MAC) of the main wing.  This ensures adequate static stability while allowing
for control authority. Because VTOL configurations often have non-uniform mass distribution due to
heavy batteries or motors, designers often iterate on the CG position relative to the Aerodynamic
Center (AC) to achieve a desired static margin.

# 1.10 Boom/Arm sizing

Arm length must give prop-tip clearance: motor-to-motor spacing ≥ 1.2–1.5 × prop diameter to avoid
adjacent rotors interfering with each other's airflow (reduces efficiency and can cause
vibration/control issues).
At D=0.433m (17"), spacing ≥ 0.52–0.65 m between adjacent motor centers.
Arms are cantilever beams under thrust reaction load in hover — size them per Section 9 below,
don't just eyeball tube diameter.
Symmetric placement about CG in both X and Y is mandatory for balanced hover — asymmetry here
shows up as constant trim correction from the flight controller, which drains battery.
