# Chapter 7 — Flight Control Systems

## 1. Introduction

A flight-control system converts the desired aircraft behavior into commands that can be executed by the aircraft actuators.

The flight-dynamics model developed in Chapter 6 describes how the aircraft responds to forces and moments.

The control system determines how those forces and moments should be generated.

The overall relationship is:

```text
Desired Flight State
        ↓
Controller
        ↓
Control Allocation
        ↓
Actuators
        ↓
Aircraft
        ↓
Aircraft Motion
        ↓
Sensors
        ↓
State Estimation
        ↓
Feedback to Controller
```

For the present hybrid VTOL UAV, the control system must operate across multiple flight regimes:

1. VTOL hover,
2. vertical climb,
3. vertical descent,
4. transition,
5. forward flight,
6. return transition,
7. landing.

The control architecture therefore cannot be treated as a conventional fixed-wing autopilot alone or as a conventional quadrotor controller alone.

---

# 2. Control-System Objectives

The main objectives are:

* stabilize aircraft attitude,
* control altitude,
* control airspeed,
* control heading,
* control position,
* maintain desired flight path,
* distribute commands among actuators,
* manage the transition between VTOL and forward flight,
* reject disturbances,
* respect actuator limits,
* maintain safe operation.

The controller should ultimately provide closed-loop control of:

$$
\phi,\theta,\psi
$$

$$
h
$$

$$
V
$$

and, during autonomous operation:

$$
x,y,z
$$

---

# 3. Aircraft Actuators

The aircraft has two major actuator groups.

## VTOL propulsion

Four independent lift motors:

$$
T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4
$$

These provide:

* vertical thrust,
* roll control,
* pitch control,
* yaw control.

## Forward propulsion

One rear-mounted pusher:

$$
T_p
$$

This provides the primary propulsive force during fixed-wing forward flight.

## Aerodynamic control surfaces

Depending on the final mechanical configuration:

* ailerons,
* elevator,
* rudder.

These primarily provide:

* roll,
* pitch,
* yaw control.

---

# 4. Control Architecture

A hierarchical architecture is suitable for the project.

```text
Mission / Navigation
        ↓
Position Controller
        ↓
Velocity Controller
        ↓
Attitude Controller
        ↓
Rate Controller
        ↓
Control Allocation
        ↓
Motors / Servos
```

The lower layers operate at higher frequencies than the upper layers.

For example:

```text
Position loop       → slow
Velocity loop       → medium
Attitude loop       → faster
Angular-rate loop   → fastest
```

The exact frequencies should be selected during implementation and validated experimentally.

---

# 5. Feedback Control

A basic feedback controller compares the desired state with the measured or estimated state.

The error is:

$$
e(t)=r(t)-y(t)
$$

where:

* \(r(t)\) = desired state,
* \(y(t)\) = measured/estimated state,
* \(e(t)\) = control error.

The controller uses this error to generate an actuator command.

For example:

$$
e_\phi=\phi_{command}-\phi
$$

and:

$$
e_h=h_{command}-h
$$

---

# 6. PID Control

A PID controller is:

$$
u(t)
=
K_Pe(t)
+
K_I\int e(t)\,dt
+
K_D\frac{de(t)}{dt}
$$

where:

* \(K_P\) = proportional gain,
* \(K_I\) = integral gain,
* \(K_D\) = derivative gain.

The three terms have different purposes.

### Proportional

$$
K_Pe
$$

Provides a response proportional to the current error.

### Integral

$$
K_I\int e\,dt
$$

Reduces persistent steady-state error.

### Derivative

$$
K_D\frac{de}{dt}
$$

Provides damping based on the rate of error change.

PID control is useful as a starting point because it is:

* simple,
* widely understood,
* computationally inexpensive,
* practical for embedded implementation.

---

# 7. Rate Control

The innermost control loop controls angular rates:

$$
p,q,r
$$

The desired rates are:

$$
p_{cmd},q_{cmd},r_{cmd}
$$

The errors are:

$$
e_p=p_{cmd}-p
$$

$$
e_q=q_{cmd}-q
$$

$$
e_r=r_{cmd}-r
$$

The rate controller then generates desired roll, pitch, and yaw moments:

$$
L_{cmd}
$$

$$
M_{cmd}
$$

$$
N_{cmd}
$$

These commands are passed to the control-allocation layer.

---

# 8. Attitude Control

The attitude controller controls:

$$
\phi,\theta,\psi
$$

The desired attitude is:

$$
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{cmd}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\phi_{cmd}\\
\theta_{cmd}\\
\psi_{cmd}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The measured/estimated attitude is:

$$
\boldsymbol{\eta}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\phi\\
\theta\\
\psi
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore:

$$
e_\eta
=
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{cmd}
-
\boldsymbol{\eta}
$$

The attitude controller converts this into desired angular rates.

```text
Desired Attitude
       ↓
Attitude Error
       ↓
Attitude Controller
       ↓
Desired Angular Rates
       ↓
Rate Controller
```

---

# 9. Nested Control Loops

A typical architecture is:

```text
Position
   ↓
Velocity
   ↓
Attitude
   ↓
Angular Rate
   ↓
Control Allocation
   ↓
Actuators
```

This nested structure separates different dynamic time scales.

The rate loop responds quickly to disturbances.

The attitude loop commands desired orientation.

The position and velocity loops operate at slower time scales.

---

# 10. VTOL Control

During hover, the four VTOL motors provide the primary control authority.

The controller produces desired:

$$
T
$$

$$
L
$$

$$
M
$$

$$
N
$$

where:

* \(T\) = total vertical thrust,
* \(L\) = roll moment,
* \(M\) = pitch moment,
* \(N\) = yaw moment.

These quantities are converted into individual motor commands.

---

# 11. Collective Thrust

The total VTOL thrust is:

$$
T_{total}
=
T_1+T_2+T_3+T_4
$$

For ideal hover:

$$
T_{total}=W
$$

For climb:

$$
T_{total}>W
$$

For descent:

$$
T_{total}<W
$$

The controller therefore changes collective thrust to control altitude.

---

# 12. Roll Control

Roll control is generated by creating a thrust difference across the lateral axis.

A simplified relationship is:

$$
L_{cmd}
=
(T_{right}-T_{left})l_y
$$

where:

* \(l_y\) = lateral motor moment arm.

The exact allocation depends on the physical motor arrangement.

A positive roll command therefore causes the control allocator to modify the four motor thrusts appropriately.

---

# 13. Pitch Control

Pitch control is produced using differential thrust between longitudinally separated motors.

A simplified relationship is:

$$
M_{cmd}
=
(T_{front}-T_{rear})l_x
$$

where:

* \(l_x\) = longitudinal motor moment arm.

Again, the actual allocation must use the measured geometry of the aircraft.

---

# 14. Yaw Control

Yaw is generated primarily by motor reaction torque.

The motor torque can be approximated as:

$$
Q_i=k_Q\omega_i^2
$$

while thrust can be approximated as:

$$
T_i=k_T\omega_i^2
$$

where:

* \(k_Q\) = torque coefficient,
* \(k_T\) = thrust coefficient,
* \(\omega_i\) = motor angular velocity.

Opposing rotor directions allow the controller to generate a net yaw moment.

The exact coefficients must come from the selected motor-propeller combination or experimental testing.

---

# 15. Control Allocation Matrix

The four VTOL motors can be represented by:

$$
\mathbf{T}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
T_1\\
T_2\\
T_3\\
T_4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The desired force/moment vector can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
T\\
L\\
M\\
N
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The allocation relationship is:

$$
\mathbf{v}=B\mathbf{T}
$$

where \(B\) depends on:

* motor locations,
* thrust directions,
* motor rotation directions,
* moment arms.

The allocation matrix must be constructed from the actual aircraft geometry.

---

# 16. Control Allocation Example

Conceptually:

```text
Desired
Total Thrust
Roll Moment
Pitch Moment
Yaw Moment
      ↓
Control Allocation Matrix
      ↓
 ┌────┬────┬────┬────┐
 │ T1 │ T2 │ T3 │ T4 │
 └────┴────┴────┴────┘
      ↓
VTOL Motors
```

The controller should not directly contain hard-coded assumptions about motor output without documenting the geometry that produced them.

---

# 17. Actuator Saturation

Each motor has a physical limit:

$$
T_{min}\leq T_i\leq T_{max}
$$

Similarly, control surfaces have limits:

$$
\delta_{min}\leq\delta\leq\delta_{max}
$$

If the requested control exceeds these limits, the command must be constrained.

Ignoring actuator saturation can cause simulation results that cannot be reproduced by the physical aircraft.

---

# 18. Anti-Windup

Integral control can continue accumulating error while an actuator is saturated.

This can cause integral windup.

For example:

```text
Large error
    ↓
Integral increases
    ↓
Actuator reaches maximum
    ↓
Integral continues increasing
    ↓
Large overshoot after saturation ends
```

An anti-windup mechanism should therefore be implemented.

Possible approaches include:

* integral clamping,
* conditional integration,
* back-calculation.

---

# 19. Forward-Flight Control

During forward flight, aerodynamic control surfaces become the primary attitude-control mechanism.

The main control inputs are:

$$
\delta_a
$$

$$
\delta_e
$$

$$
\delta_r
$$

and the rear pusher throttle:

$$
T_p
$$

The control architecture therefore changes from primarily thrust-vector control to aerodynamic control.

---

# 20. Aileron Control

Ailerons primarily control roll.

The desired roll moment is:

$$
L_{cmd}
$$

and the aileron command is:

$$
\delta_a
$$

A simplified relationship is:

$$
C_l
=
C_{l0}
+
C_{l\beta}\beta
+
C_{lp}\frac{pb}{2V}
+
C_{l\delta_a}\delta_a
$$

Therefore:

$$
L
=
q_\infty SbC_l
$$

The actual aerodynamic derivative values should be obtained from aerodynamic analysis or system identification.

---

# 21. Elevator Control

The elevator primarily controls pitch.

A simplified pitching-moment model is:

$$
C_m
=
C_{m0}
+
C_{m\alpha}\alpha
+
C_{mq}\frac{qc}{2V}
+
C_{m\delta_e}\delta_e
$$

The resulting pitching moment is:

$$
M=q_\infty ScC_m
$$

The elevator controller therefore modifies:

$$
\delta_e
$$

to achieve the desired pitch behavior.

---

# 22. Rudder Control

The rudder primarily controls yaw.

A simplified model is:

$$
C_n
=
C_{n0}
+
C_{n\beta}\beta
+
C_{nr}\frac{rb}{2V}
+
C_{n\delta_r}\delta_r
$$

and:

$$
N=q_\infty SbC_n
$$

The rudder is particularly useful for:

* heading control,
* coordinated turns,
* sideslip correction,
* disturbance rejection.

---

# 23. Pusher Throttle Control

The rear pusher provides forward thrust:

$$
T_p
$$

A simplified forward-flight force equation is:

$$
m\dot{u}
=
T_p-D+\text{other force components}
$$

Therefore:

* increasing \(T_p\) generally increases forward acceleration,
* decreasing \(T_p\) reduces forward acceleration.

The actual response depends on:

* aircraft drag,
* mass,
* propeller performance,
* motor response,
* airspeed.

---

# 24. Airspeed Control

Airspeed control compares:

$$
V_{cmd}
$$

with:

$$
V
$$

The error is:

$$
e_V=V_{cmd}-V
$$

The controller can generate a desired throttle or propulsion command.

Depending on the selected control architecture, pitch may also participate in airspeed/energy management.

The final control allocation should be determined after the aircraft's longitudinal dynamics are identified.

---

# 25. Altitude Control

Altitude is represented as:

$$
h
$$

The altitude error is:

$$
e_h=h_{cmd}-h
$$

The altitude controller generates a desired vertical velocity or thrust/flight-path command.

A hierarchical structure is:

```text
Desired Altitude
       ↓
Altitude Controller
       ↓
Desired Vertical Velocity
       ↓
Velocity Controller
       ↓
Desired Thrust / Attitude
       ↓
Actuator Commands
```

The implementation differs between VTOL and forward flight.

---

# 26. Position Control

For autonomous flight, the aircraft must control position:

$$
x,y,z
$$

The position error is:

$$
\mathbf{e}_p
=
\mathbf{p}_{cmd}
-
\mathbf{p}
$$

The position controller generates a desired velocity:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{cmd}
$$

The velocity controller then generates desired acceleration or attitude commands.

```text
Position
   ↓
Velocity
   ↓
Acceleration
   ↓
Attitude / Thrust
   ↓
Aircraft
```

---

# 27. Heading Control

Heading is related to yaw:

$$
\psi
$$

The heading error should account for angle wrapping.

For example, a direct subtraction can produce an incorrect large error near:

$$
0^\circ/360^\circ
$$

Therefore, heading error should be normalized to a selected interval such as:

$$
[-180^\circ,180^\circ]
$$

---

# 28. Cascaded Position Controller

A complete autonomous controller may use:

```text
Position Controller
        ↓
Velocity Controller
        ↓
Attitude Controller
        ↓
Rate Controller
        ↓
Control Allocation
        ↓
Actuators
```

This structure allows each layer to have a clearly defined purpose.

---

Possible states include:

SAFE
 ↓
VTOL_IDLE
 ↓
VTOL_TAKEOFF
 ↓
VTOL_HOVER
 ↓
TRANSITION_TO_FORWARD
 ↓
FORWARD_FLIGHT
 ↓
TRANSITION_TO_VTOL

VTOL_LANDING
 ↓

# 30. Mode Switching

A flight-mode manager determines which control laws are active.

For example:

### VTOL mode

Active:

* VTOL attitude controller,
* VTOL altitude controller,
* four-motor control allocation.

### Forward-flight mode

Active:

* fixed-wing attitude controller,
* airspeed controller,
* aerodynamic control surfaces,
* pusher throttle.

### Transition mode

Active:

* blended control system,
* pusher,
* VTOL motors,
* aerodynamic surfaces.

---

# 31. Transition Control

Transition is the most complex control regime.

During transition:

$$
T_{VTOL}
$$

and:

$$
T_p
$$

are both relevant.

At the same time:

$$
L_{wing}
$$

increases with airspeed.

The controller therefore needs to manage the changing balance between:

* VTOL thrust,
* aerodynamic lift,
* pusher thrust,
* aircraft attitude.

---

# 32. Control Blending

A transition parameter can be defined as:

$$
\lambda\in[0,1]
$$

where:

$$
\lambda=0
$$

represents VTOL-dominant control and:

$$
\lambda=1
$$

represents forward-flight-dominant control.

A simplified blended command is:

$$
u
=
(1-\lambda)u_{VTOL}
+
\lambda u_{FW}
$$

where:

* \(u_{VTOL}\) = VTOL controller output,
* \(u_{FW}\) = fixed-wing controller output.

This is a conceptual framework.

The actual transition logic should be developed from simulation and aircraft behavior rather than assuming a simple linear blend is sufficient.

---

# 33. Transition Scheduling Variables

Possible scheduling variables include:

* airspeed,
* pitch angle,
* dynamic pressure,
* wing lift estimate,
* time since transition started,
* VTOL thrust requirement.

Airspeed is particularly useful because aerodynamic control authority increases with dynamic pressure:

$$
q_\infty=\frac12\rho V^2
$$

As \(V\) increases, aerodynamic control effectiveness generally increases.

---

# 34. Transition Safety Conditions

A transition should not be initiated blindly.

Possible conditions include:

```text
Aircraft stable
      AND
Sufficient altitude
      AND
Sensors valid
      AND
Battery sufficient
      AND
Pusher available
      AND
No critical fault
```

The exact safety logic should be defined later according to the aircraft and flight-test plan.

---

# 35. Return Transition

The reverse transition is also important.

A possible sequence is:

```text
Forward Flight
      ↓
Reduce forward speed
      ↓
Enable/increase VTOL thrust
      ↓
Establish VTOL attitude
      ↓
Reduce pusher thrust
      ↓
Enter hover
      ↓
Vertical descent
```

The return transition should be tested separately from the initial transition.

---

# 36. Disturbance Rejection

The control system should compensate for disturbances such as:

* wind,
* gusts,
* asymmetric thrust,
* motor mismatch,
* payload changes,
* actuator errors.

For example, if a gust produces a roll disturbance:

$$
L_{disturbance}\neq0
$$

the attitude controller should detect:

$$
\phi\neq\phi_{cmd}
$$

and command a corrective roll moment.

---

# 37. Sensor Inputs

The controller does not directly measure all aircraft states.

Potential sensors include:

* IMU,
* gyroscope,
* accelerometer,
* magnetometer,
* barometer,
* GNSS,
* airspeed sensor,
* optical-flow sensor,
* range sensor.

The exact sensor suite should be selected in the electronics and avionics design stage.

---

# 38. State Estimation

Sensor measurements are combined to estimate aircraft state.

The estimator can provide:

$$
\hat{x}
$$

rather than the true state:

$$
x
$$

For example:

$$
\hat{\phi},\hat{\theta},\hat{\psi}
$$

are estimated attitude angles.

The controller operates using these estimates.

---

# 39. Complementary Filter

A simple attitude-estimation approach is a complementary filter.

Conceptually:

```text
Gyroscope
    ↓
Fast attitude information
    │
    ├──────► Fusion ◄────── Accelerometer
    │
    ↓
Estimated attitude
```

Gyroscopes provide excellent short-term angular-rate information but accumulate drift.

Accelerometers provide a gravity reference but are affected by aircraft acceleration.

A complementary filter combines their strengths.

---

# 40. Kalman Filtering

For a more advanced estimator, a Kalman-filter-based approach can be used.

A general state model is:

$$
x_{k+1}=f(x_k,u_k)+w_k
$$

and:

$$
y_k=h(x_k)+v_k
$$

where:

* \(w_k\) = process noise,
* \(v_k\) = measurement noise.

An Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) can be used when the system is nonlinear.

The estimator can combine:

* IMU,
* GNSS,
* barometer,
* magnetometer,
* airspeed,
* other sensors.

---

# 41. Control-Loop Timing

Different loops should operate at different update rates.

A conceptual arrangement is:

```text
Rate Controller       → highest frequency
Attitude Controller   → high frequency
Velocity Controller   → medium frequency
Position Controller   → lower frequency
Navigation            → lower frequency
Mission Planning      → lowest frequency
```

The actual frequencies should be selected based on:

* aircraft dynamics,
* processor capability,
* sensor update rates,
* actuator response,
* controller bandwidth.

They should not be chosen arbitrarily.

---

# 42. Controller Tuning

Controller tuning should follow a systematic process.

A basic sequence is:

```text
Model Aircraft
     ↓
Determine Dynamic Response
     ↓
Initial Controller Gains
     ↓
Simulation
     ↓
Check Stability
     ↓
Adjust Gains
     ↓
Hardware-in-the-Loop
     ↓
Ground Testing
     ↓
Low-Risk Flight Testing
```

The aircraft should not be tuned directly through uncontrolled flight experiments.

---

# 43. Step Response

A step input can be used to evaluate controller behavior.

For example:

$$
\phi_{cmd}:0^\circ\rightarrow10^\circ
$$

The response can be evaluated using:

* rise time,
* settling time,
* overshoot,
* steady-state error,
* oscillation,
* control effort.

The same principle can be applied to:

* pitch,
* yaw,
* altitude,
* airspeed.

---

# 44. Controller Performance Metrics

Important metrics include:

### Rise time

Time required for the response to reach the desired region.

### Settling time

Time required for the response to remain within a specified error band.

### Overshoot

Maximum amount by which the response exceeds the target.

### Steady-state error

Difference remaining after the transient has settled.

### RMS tracking error

$$
RMSE=
\sqrt{
\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_i^2
}
$$

These metrics should be reported in `Results.md` when actual controller testing is performed.

---

# 45. Control Effort

Controller performance should not be evaluated only by tracking accuracy.

The actuator effort is also important.

For example:

$$
J_u=\int u^2(t)\,dt
$$

can be used as a simple measure of control effort.

Excessive control activity may indicate:

* poor tuning,
* noisy measurements,
* insufficient damping,
* actuator limitations,
* an inaccurate model.

---

# 46. Rate Limits

Real actuators cannot change instantaneously.

Motor and servo commands should therefore include rate limits where appropriate.

For example:

$$
\left|\frac{d\delta}{dt}\right|
\leq
\dot{\delta}_{max}
$$

This produces more realistic simulation behavior.

---

# 47. Dead Zones

Some actuators may have a small region in which command changes do not produce significant physical response.

Examples include:

* servo backlash,
* ESC command dead zones,
* motor startup behavior.

If measured during testing, these effects can be incorporated into the actuator model.

---

# 48. Motor Mismatch

The four VTOL motors will not necessarily generate exactly equal thrust for equal commands.

Differences can result from:

* motor manufacturing variation,
* propeller variation,
* ESC variation,
* battery voltage,
* installation differences.

Therefore:

$$
T_1\neq T_2\neq T_3\neq T_4
$$

may occur even when the commands are nominally identical.

Calibration can reduce this mismatch.

---

# 49. Motor Calibration

A practical motor calibration procedure can measure:

$$
PWM
\rightarrow
RPM
\rightarrow
Thrust
$$

or an equivalent command-to-thrust relationship.

A fitted model might be:

$$
T=a u^2+bu+c
$$

where \(u\) is the actuator command.

The actual relationship should be obtained experimentally.

---

# 50. Servo Calibration

Control surfaces should also be calibrated.

For example:

$$
u_{servo}
\rightarrow
\delta_{actual}
$$

The measured relationship should account for:

* mechanical limits,
* neutral position,
* direction,
* linkage geometry.

This ensures that the control model matches the physical aircraft.

---

# 51. Failsafe Control

The control system should define responses to major failures.

Possible failures include:

* loss of GNSS,
* sensor failure,
* low battery,
* motor failure,
* communication loss,
* estimator failure,
* controller fault.

The appropriate response depends on the aircraft configuration and operating condition.

For example, a loss of a single VTOL motor during hover may produce an immediate control-authority problem that cannot simply be handled by increasing all remaining motors.

Failure analysis should therefore be performed before flight.

---

# 52. Control Authority Analysis

The controller should determine whether the aircraft can generate sufficient corrective moments.

For each axis:

$$
M_{available}
$$

should be compared with:

$$
M_{required}
$$

For example:

$$
L_{available}>L_{required}
$$

should hold for the intended maneuver within the design envelope.

This should be checked under:

* normal battery voltage,
* low battery voltage,
* maximum mass,
* minimum mass,
* motor mismatch,
* wind disturbances.

---

# 53. Control Allocation with Constraints

The control-allocation problem can be written as:

$$
\min_{\mathbf{u}}
\left\|
B\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{desired}
\right\|^2
$$

subject to:

$$
u_{min}\leq u_i\leq u_{max}
$$

This becomes useful when:

* actuators saturate,
* multiple actuators can produce the same moment,
* some actuators fail,
* actuator priorities are required.

A constrained allocator can therefore be more robust than a simple matrix inverse.

---

# 54. Control Architecture During Flight Modes

A simplified architecture is:

| Flight Mode       | Primary Control                      |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Hover             | Four VTOL motors                     |
| Vertical climb    | Four VTOL motors                     |
| Transition        | VTOL + pusher + aerodynamic surfaces |
| Forward flight    | Rear pusher + aerodynamic surfaces   |
| Return transition | VTOL + pusher + aerodynamic surfaces |
| Landing           | Four VTOL motors                     |

The exact actuator blending must be determined experimentally.

---

# 55. Control-System Simulation

Before hardware testing, the controller should be tested in simulation.

The simulation loop is:

```text
Reference
   ↓
Controller
   ↓
Actuator Model
   ↓
6-DOF Aircraft Model
   ↓
Sensor Model
   ↓
State Estimator
   ↓
Controller
```

This creates a closed-loop simulation.

---

# 56. Disturbance Simulation

The controller should be tested against disturbances.

Examples:

### Wind

$$
V_{wind}\neq0
$$

### Gust

A temporary change in wind velocity.

### Motor mismatch

$$
T_i=T_{nominal}+\Delta T_i
$$

### CG shift

$$
x_{CG}\rightarrow x_{CG}+\Delta x
$$

### Sensor noise

$$
y_{measured}=y_{true}+n
$$

These tests determine whether the controller remains stable and usable under non-ideal conditions.

---

# 57. Hardware-in-the-Loop Testing

After software simulation, the next step can be hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing.

Conceptually:

```text
Real Flight Controller
        ↓
Real-Time Interface
        ↓
Simulated Aircraft
        ↓
Simulated Sensors
        ↓
Real Flight Controller
```

This allows embedded software to be tested without immediately flying the aircraft.

---

# 58. Software-in-the-Loop Testing

Software-in-the-loop (SITL) allows the control software to communicate with a simulated aircraft model.

It is useful for:

* debugging,
* controller tuning,
* mission testing,

# 66. Important Control-System Engineering Distinctions

The following terms should remain separate in the project.

### Command

What the controller requests.

### Estimate

### Control error

Difference between desired and estimated state.

### Controller output

The desired force, moment, or actuator command.

### Actuator output

# 67. Common Control-System Failure Modes

Potential problems include:

### Incorrect sign convention

A corrective command may make the aircraft error larger instead of smaller.

### Excessive gain

Can produce oscillation or instability.

### Insufficient gain

Can produce slow or weak response.

### Integral windup

Can produce excessive overshoot.

### Sensor noise

Can cause actuator oscillation.

### Actuator saturation

Can prevent the aircraft from generating the required control moment.

### Incorrect control allocation

Can produce the wrong aircraft response.

### Poor transition scheduling

Can cause loss of control authority during mode switching.

These should be considered during simulation and testing.

# 74. Summary

The flight-control system converts desired aircraft behavior into physical actuator commands.

For this hybrid VTOL aircraft, control must account for two fundamentally different propulsion/control systems:

$$
\boxed{
4\ VTOL\ Lift\ Motors
+
1\ Rear\ Pusher
+
Aerodynamic\ Control\ Surfaces
}
$$

During hover:

$$
T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4
$$

provide the primary control authority.

During forward flight:

$$
T_p
$$

provides forward propulsion while:

$$
\delta_a,\delta_e,\delta_r
$$

provide the primary aerodynamic attitude control.

During transition, the systems must work together.

The overall control architecture is:

$$
\boxed{
Reference
\rightarrow
State\ Estimation
\rightarrow
Controller
\rightarrow
Control\ Allocation
\rightarrow
Actuators
\rightarrow
Aircraft
\rightarrow
Sensors
\rightarrow
Feedback
}
$$

The development process should follow:

$$
\boxed{
Model
\rightarrow
Simulate
\rightarrow
Tune
\rightarrow
SITL
\rightarrow
HIL
\rightarrow
Ground\ Test
\rightarrow
Flight\ Test
\rightarrow
Validate
}
$$

The purpose of this chapter is therefore not simply to select a PID controller. It establishes the complete control architecture required to operate the aircraft across VTOL, transition, and forward-flight regimes.
