# Chapter 6 — Flight Dynamics

## 1. Introduction

Flight dynamics is the study of how an aircraft moves, rotates, responds to external forces, and reacts to control inputs.

For the present project, flight dynamics connects the previously developed:

* aircraft geometry,
* mass properties,
* aerodynamic model,
* propulsion system,
* structural configuration,
* center-of-gravity location,
* control surfaces,
* VTOL motors,
* rear pusher motor,

into a mathematical model of aircraft motion.

The objective of this chapter is not simply to calculate whether the aircraft can fly. The objective is to determine:

1. how the aircraft moves through space,
2. how it rotates about its body axes,
3. how aerodynamic forces and moments are generated,
4. how the four VTOL motors affect aircraft motion,
5. how the rear pusher affects forward-flight motion,
6. how control inputs affect the aircraft,
7. how the aircraft behaves in different flight modes,
8. how the aircraft can be simulated before flight testing,
9. how the mathematical model can eventually be compared with experimental data.

The flight-dynamics model will later provide the foundation for:

* autopilot development,
* attitude control,
* position control,
* transition control,
* trajectory following,
* obstacle avoidance,
* simulation,
* system identification,
* hardware-in-the-loop testing.

---

# 2. Aircraft Configuration

The aircraft is a fixed-wing hybrid VTOL UAV using a lift-plus-cruise architecture.

The propulsion configuration consists of:

* four dedicated VTOL lift motors,
* one independent rear-mounted pusher motor.

The four VTOL motors are primarily responsible for vertical flight.

The rear pusher motor is primarily responsible for forward fixed-wing flight.

During forward flight, the main wing generates the majority of the aircraft lift while the rear pusher provides the required forward thrust.

A simplified representation is:

```text
                 MAIN WING
        ─────────────────────────
          ↑                    ↑
       VTOL motor          VTOL motor
          │                    │
          │                    │

             ┌─────────────┐
             │  FUSELAGE  │──────────────►
             └─────────────┘        Rear pusher
                  │
                  │
              Tail surfaces
```

The aircraft therefore operates in several distinct dynamic conditions:

```text
VTOL / Hover
     ↓
Vertical Climb
     ↓
Transition
     ↓
Forward Flight
     ↓
Transition to VTOL
     ↓
Vertical Descent
     ↓
Landing
```

These conditions cannot be represented perfectly by one simplified aerodynamic model. The forces and dominant control mechanisms change between flight modes.

# 3. Objectives of Flight-Dynamics Analysis

The main objectives are:

### 3.1 Determine aircraft motion

Determine:

* translational velocity,
* acceleration,
* angular velocity,
* angular acceleration,
* attitude,
* position.

### 3.2 Determine forces

Calculate contributions from:

* gravity,
* aerodynamic lift,
* aerodynamic drag,
* aerodynamic side force,
* VTOL thrust,
* pusher thrust,
* other external forces.

### 3.3 Determine moments

Calculate moments generated about the aircraft center of gravity by:

* aerodynamic forces,
* control surfaces,
* VTOL motors,
* pusher thrust,
* gravity offset,
* asymmetric thrust.

### 3.4 Develop a mathematical model

The aircraft should eventually be represented using a six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body model.

### 3.5 Provide a foundation for control

The resulting model will later be used to develop:

* attitude control,
* altitude control,
* heading control,
* velocity control,
* transition control,
* trajectory control.

# 4. Coordinate Systems

A flight-dynamics model requires clearly defined coordinate systems.

Two important coordinate systems are:

1. inertial/world frame,
2. aircraft body frame.

---

# 5. Inertial / World Coordinate Frame

The inertial frame describes the aircraft position relative to the Earth or simulation environment.

A commonly used convention is:

* \(X\): forward/reference horizontal direction,
* \(Y\): lateral direction,
* \(Z\): vertical direction.

The exact sign convention must be documented because different simulation environments use different conventions.

For example, an Earth-fixed frame may use:

$$
\mathbf{r}=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y\\
z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where:

* \(x\) = aircraft position along the first world axis,
* \(y\) = lateral position,
* \(z\) = vertical position.

The position vector is:

$$
\mathbf{r}=
\begin{bmatrix}
x & y & z
\end{bmatrix}^{T}
$$

---

# 6. Aircraft Body Coordinate Frame

The body frame is attached to the aircraft.

A common aircraft convention is:

* \(x_b\): forward,
* \(y_b\): right,
* \(z_b\): downward.

Thus:

```text
                    +Xb
                     ↑
                     │
                     │
          +Yb  ◄──── UAV ────►  -Yb
                     │
                     ↓
                    +Zb
```

The body frame is particularly useful because:

* aerodynamic forces are naturally expressed relative to the aircraft,
* propulsion forces act along known body directions,
* moments are measured about the aircraft center of gravity,
* inertia properties are defined in the body frame.

---

# 7. Six Degrees of Freedom

A rigid aircraft has six fundamental degrees of freedom.

These consist of:

### Translational motion

1. motion along \(x\),
2. motion along \(y\),
3. motion along \(z\).

### Rotational motion

4. roll,
5. pitch,
6. yaw.

The three rotational angles are commonly represented as:

* roll angle \(\phi\),
* pitch angle \(\theta\),
* yaw angle \(\psi\).

The complete aircraft state can therefore include:

$$
[x,y,z,u,v,w,\phi,\theta,\psi,p,q,r]
$$

where:

* \(x,y,z\) = position,
* \(u,v,w\) = body-axis velocity components,
* \(\phi,\theta,\psi\) = attitude angles,
* \(p,q,r\) = body angular velocities.

This forms the basis of a six-degree-of-freedom model.

---

# 8. Translational Velocity

The aircraft velocity in the body frame can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{V}_b=
\begin{bmatrix}
u\\
v\\
w
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where:

* \(u\) = forward body-axis velocity,
* \(v\) = lateral body-axis velocity,
* \(w\) = vertical body-axis velocity.

The total airspeed is:

$$
V=\sqrt{u^2+v^2+w^2}
$$

For straight and level forward flight, a simplified approximation is:

$$
u\approx V
$$

and:

$$
v\approx0
$$

$$
w\approx0
$$

For hover and transition, this simplification is no longer valid.

---

# 9. Angular Velocity

The angular velocity of the aircraft is represented by:

$$
\boldsymbol{\omega}=
\begin{bmatrix}
p\\
q\\
r
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where:

* \(p\) = roll rate,
* \(q\) = pitch rate,
* \(r\) = yaw rate.

These quantities describe how quickly the aircraft rotates about its body axes.

For example:

* large \(p\) → rapid roll motion,
* large \(q\) → rapid pitch motion,
* large \(r\) → rapid yaw motion.

---

# 10. Euler Angles

Aircraft orientation can be represented using Euler angles:

$$
\phi,\theta,\psi
$$

where:

### Roll

$$
\phi
$$

Rotation about the body \(x\)-axis.

### Pitch

$$
\theta
$$

Rotation about the body \(y\)-axis.

### Yaw

$$
\psi
$$

Rotation about the vertical/reference axis.

A simplified interpretation is:

```text
Roll   → wing-to-wing rotation
Pitch  → nose/tail rotation
Yaw    → heading rotation
```

Euler angles are convenient for visualization and interpretation.

However, they have a mathematical singularity near:

$$
\theta=\pm90^\circ
$$

Therefore, quaternions may be preferred for full nonlinear simulation.

---

# 11. Quaternion Representation

A quaternion can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{q}=
\begin{bmatrix}
q_0\\
q_1\\
q_2\\
q_3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

with the normalization condition:

$$
q_0^2+q_1^2+q_2^2+q_3^2=1
$$

Quaternions avoid the Euler-angle singularity associated with pitch approaching \(90^\circ\).

They are therefore useful for:

* flight simulation,
* attitude estimation,
* autopilot software,
* aggressive maneuvers,
* transition simulation.

For early analytical work, Euler angles are easier to understand. For implementation, quaternions can be used internally.

---

# 12. Newton's Second Law

The translational motion of the aircraft follows Newton's second law:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}
$$

where:

* \(\mathbf{F}\) = total external force,
* \(m\) = aircraft mass,
* \(\mathbf{a}\) = acceleration.

For the aircraft:

$$
\mathbf{F}_{total}
=
\mathbf{F}_{aero}
+
\mathbf{F}_{prop}
+
\mathbf{F}_{gravity}
+
\mathbf{F}_{other}
$$

The major force categories are therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf{F}_{total}
=
\mathbf{F}_{aero}
+
\mathbf{F}_{VTOL}
+
\mathbf{F}_{pusher}
+
\mathbf{F}_{gravity}
}
$$

during a simplified model.

---

# 13. Gravity

The aircraft weight is:

$$
W=mg
$$

For the preliminary aircraft mass assumption:

$$
m=5\,kg
$$

and:

$$
g=9.81\,m/s^2
$$

therefore:

$$
W=5(9.81)
$$

$$
\boxed{W\approx49.05\,N}
$$

Gravity acts through the aircraft center of gravity.

In body coordinates, the apparent components of gravity change as the aircraft rotates.

Therefore, gravity contributes differently to the body-axis force equations depending on:

$$
\phi,\theta,\psi
$$

---

# 14. Body-Axis Translational Equations

A standard rigid-body formulation can be written approximately as:

$$
m(\dot{u}+qw-rv)=X-mg\sin\theta
$$

$$
m(\dot{v}+ru-pw)=Y+mg\sin\phi\cos\theta
$$

$$
m(\dot{w}+pv-qu)=Z+mg\cos\phi\cos\theta
$$

where:

* \(X\) = total body-axis force in \(x\),
* \(Y\) = total body-axis force in \(y\),
* \(Z\) = total body-axis force in \(z\).

These equations contain the coupling between translational and rotational motion.

For example:

$$
qw-rv
$$

represents coupling caused by aircraft angular motion.

This is one reason why aircraft dynamics cannot always be treated as independent one-dimensional problems.

---

# 15. Rotational Dynamics

The rotational motion follows:

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}
=
\frac{d\mathbf{H}}{dt}
$$

where:

* \(\mathbf{M}\) = total moment,
* \(\mathbf{H}\) = angular momentum.

For a rigid aircraft:

$$
\mathbf{H}=I\boldsymbol{\omega}
$$

where \(I\) is the inertia matrix.

The rotational equations can be written as:

$$
I\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
+
\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(I\boldsymbol{\omega})
=
\mathbf{M}
$$

This is the general rigid-body rotational equation.

---

# 16. Aircraft Inertia Matrix

The inertia matrix is:

$$
I=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_{xx} & -I_{xy} & -I_{xz}\\
-I_{xy} & I_{yy} & -I_{yz}\\
-I_{xz} & -I_{yz} & I_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where:

* \(I_{xx}\) = roll-axis moment of inertia,
* \(I_{yy}\) = pitch-axis moment of inertia,
* \(I_{zz}\) = yaw-axis moment of inertia,
* \(I_{xy},I_{xz},I_{yz}\) = products of inertia.

For a sufficiently symmetric aircraft with appropriately selected body axes, the products of inertia may be small.

However, they should not automatically be assumed to be zero.

The final values should be obtained from the CAD model or experimental identification.

---

# 17. Moment Equations

The simplified rotational equations are commonly represented as:

$$
I_{xx}\dot p + \ldots = L
$$

$$
I_{yy}\dot q + \ldots = M
$$

$$
I_{zz}\dot r + \ldots = N
$$

where:

* \(L\) = roll moment,
* \(M\) = pitch moment,
* \(N\) = yaw moment.

A more complete body-axis form is:

$$
I_{xx}\dot p
-
I_{xy}\dot q
-
I_{xz}\dot r
+
(I_{zz}-I_{yy})qr
+
I_{yz}(q^2-r^2)
+
I_{xy}pq
-
I_{xz}pr
=L
$$

with corresponding equations for the other axes.

The exact form used in simulation must be consistent with the selected coordinate convention.

---

# 18. Aerodynamic Forces

The aerodynamic forces are generally:

* lift \(L\),
* drag \(D\),
* side force \(Y_a\).

Lift is approximately:

$$
L=\frac12\rho V^2SC_L
$$

Drag is:

$$
D=\frac12\rho V^2SC_D
$$

Dynamic pressure is:

$$
q_\infty=\frac12\rho V^2
$$

Therefore:

$$
L=q_\infty SC_L
$$

$$
D=q_\infty SC_D
$$

These equations connect directly to the aerodynamic analysis performed in Chapter 2.

---

# 19. Angle of Attack

Angle of attack is approximately:

$$
\alpha=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{w}{u}\right)
$$

for the basic body-axis definition.

A more complete definition should account for the aircraft velocity vector relative to the air.

Angle of attack strongly affects:

* lift,
* drag,
* pitching moment,
* stall behavior.

The aerodynamic coefficients can therefore be represented as functions of:

$$
C_L=C_L(\alpha)
$$

$$
C_D=C_D(\alpha)
$$

$$
C_m=C_m(\alpha)
$$

---

# 20. Sideslip Angle

The sideslip angle can be approximated by:

$$
\beta=
\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)
$$

or, for small angles:

$$
\beta\approx\frac{v}{V}
$$

Sideslip affects:

* side force,
* rolling moment,
* yawing moment.

Therefore:

$$
C_Y=C_Y(\beta)
$$

$$
C_l=C_l(\beta)
$$

$$
C_n=C_n(\beta)
$$

where:

* \(C_Y\) = side-force coefficient,
* \(C_l\) = rolling-moment coefficient,
* \(C_n\) = yawing-moment coefficient.

---

# 21. Aerodynamic Moments

Aerodynamic moments are calculated using:

$$
M=q_\infty S c C_m
$$

where:

* \(c\) = reference mean aerodynamic chord,
* \(C_m\) = pitching-moment coefficient.

Similarly:

$$
L_{moment}=q_\infty SbC_l
$$

$$
N=q_\infty SbC_n
$$

where:

* \(b\) = wingspan,
* \(C_l\) = rolling-moment coefficient,
* \(C_n\) = yawing-moment coefficient.

These coefficients can eventually be obtained from aerodynamic software or flight testing.

---

# 22. Control Surfaces

The fixed-wing aircraft can use aerodynamic control surfaces such as:

* ailerons,
* elevator,
* rudder.

Their primary effects are:

| Control  | Primary effect |
| -------- | -------------- |
| Aileron  | Roll           |
| Elevator | Pitch          |
| Rudder   | Yaw            |

However, the actual aircraft response is coupled.

For example, an aileron command can generate:

* roll,
* induced yaw,
* changes in lift distribution.

Therefore, the control model should eventually include cross-coupling.

---

# 23. Control Derivatives

A linearized aerodynamic model can use stability and control derivatives.

For example:

$$
C_L=C_{L0}+C_{L\alpha}\alpha+C_{Lq}\frac{qc}{2V}+C_{L\delta_e}\delta_e
$$

where:

* \(C_{L0}\) = zero-angle lift coefficient,
* \(C_{L\alpha}\) = lift-curve slope,
* \(C_{Lq}\) = pitch-rate derivative,
* \(\delta_e\) = elevator deflection,
* \(C_{L\delta_e}\) = elevator control derivative.

Similarly, pitching moment can be represented as:

$$
C_m
=
C_{m0}
+
C_{m\alpha}\alpha
+
C_{mq}\frac{qc}{2V}
+
C_{m\delta_e}\delta_e
$$

The same principle can be applied to lateral-directional dynamics.

---

# 24. Stability Derivatives

Important derivatives include:

### Longitudinal

$$
C_{L\alpha}
$$

$$
C_{m\alpha}
$$

$$
C_{mq}
$$

$$
C_{m\delta_e}
$$

### Lateral-directional

$$
C_{Y\beta}
$$

$$
C_{l\beta}
$$

$$
C_{l_p}
$$

$$
C_{n\beta}
$$

$$
C_{n_r}
$$

These derivatives describe how aerodynamic coefficients change when the aircraft state changes.

They are important for determining:

* static stability,
* dynamic stability,
* control authority,
* damping.

---

# 25. Static Stability

For longitudinal stability, the pitching-moment coefficient should generally decrease with increasing angle of attack around the trim condition.

A commonly examined condition is:

$$
\boxed{C_{m\alpha}<0}
$$

for conventional static longitudinal stability under the selected sign convention.

The exact interpretation depends on the coordinate and coefficient definitions used.

The CG position is therefore important.

---

# 26. Center of Gravity

The center of gravity is:

$$
x_{CG}
=
\frac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i}
$$

The CG affects:

* pitching moment,
* stability,
* control authority,
* trim,
* transition behavior,
* motor loading.

For the preliminary design, the CG target was placed around:

$$
25\%-30\%
$$

of mean aerodynamic chord.

This remains a preliminary target until the complete CAD mass properties and actual component masses are known.

---

# 27. Neutral Point

The neutral point is the longitudinal location at which the aircraft has approximately neutral static longitudinal stability.

Static margin can be represented as:

$$
SM=
\frac{x_{NP}-x_{CG}}{c}
$$

where:

* \(x_{NP}\) = neutral-point location,
* \(x_{CG}\) = center-of-gravity location,
* \(c\) = reference chord.

A positive static margin under the selected convention generally indicates that the CG is ahead of the neutral point.

The actual neutral point must be determined from the complete aircraft aerodynamic model.

---

# 28. Trim Condition

An aircraft is trimmed when the forces and moments are balanced for a selected flight condition.

For steady level forward flight:

$$
L\approx W
$$

$$
T\approx D
$$

and:

$$
M\approx0
$$

Therefore, a trim condition can be represented as:

$$
\dot{u}\approx0
$$

$$
\dot{w}\approx0
$$

$$
\dot{q}\approx0
$$

with the appropriate steady-state attitude and control inputs.

Trim analysis is important because control-system design normally begins around one or more known operating points.

---

# 29. Forward-Flight Dynamics

During forward flight, the rear pusher motor supplies the primary propulsive force.

The main wing provides most of the aerodynamic lift.

A simplified force balance is:

$$
L\approx W
$$

$$
T_{pusher}\approx D
$$

The aircraft therefore behaves approximately like a conventional fixed-wing aircraft in this flight regime, while still carrying the additional VTOL hardware.

The pusher thrust acts along its installation direction and its moment depends on its location relative to the CG.

---

# 30. Rear Pusher Thrust Moment

If the rear pusher thrust line does not pass exactly through the CG, it can produce a pitching moment.

The moment is:

$$
\mathbf{M}_{T}
=
\mathbf{r}_{T}\times\mathbf{T}_{pusher}
$$

where:

* \(\mathbf{r}_{T}\) = vector from CG to pusher thrust application point,
* \(\mathbf{T}_{pusher}\) = pusher thrust vector.

For a simplified perpendicular geometry:

$$
M_T=T_{pusher}d
$$

where \(d\) is the perpendicular moment arm.

This moment must be included in trim analysis.

---

# 31. VTOL Flight Dynamics

During hover, the four VTOL motors generate the majority of the vertical force.

The total thrust is:

$$
T_{VTOL}
=
T_1+T_2+T_3+T_4
$$

For hover:

$$
T_{VTOL}\approx W
$$

For the preliminary design, a thrust-to-weight ratio of approximately:

$$
1.8
$$

was used as a sizing target.

Therefore:

$$
T_{max,total}\approx1.8W
$$

Using:

$$
W=49.05\,N
$$

gives:

$$
T_{max,total}\approx88.3\,N
$$

With four motors:

$$
T_{motor}\approx\frac{88.3}{4}
$$

$$
\boxed{T_{motor}\approx22.1\,N}
$$

This is a preliminary sizing requirement rather than a validated motor operating point.

---

# 32. VTOL Motor Allocation

The four motors can be represented as:

$$
T_1,\;T_2,\;T_3,\;T_4
$$

Their locations relative to the CG determine their ability to generate moments.

For example:

```text
                 FRONT

              T1       T2
               ↑       ↑
               │       │

                 CG
                  ●

               │       │
               ↑       ↑
              T3       T4

                 REAR
```

The exact motor numbering and rotation directions must be fixed in the aircraft configuration and control allocation documentation.

---

# 33. Vertical Force Control

For vertical motion, the simplified force balance is:

$$
F_z=T_{VTOL}-W
$$

Therefore:

### Hover

$$
T_{VTOL}=W
$$

### Climb

$$
T_{VTOL}>W
$$

### Descent

$$
T_{VTOL}<W
$$

This is a simplified model because aerodynamic effects, rotor inflow, attitude, and vertical velocity can influence the actual force.

---

# 34. VTOL Roll Control

Roll can be generated by creating differential thrust between the left and right motor groups.

For example:

$$
T_{left}\neq T_{right}
$$

The resulting moment is approximately:

$$
M_x=\sum_i r_{y,i}T_i
$$

where:

* \(r_{y,i}\) = lateral distance of motor \(i\) from the CG,
* \(T_i\) = thrust from motor \(i\).

The controller therefore does not command individual motor thrust arbitrarily. It generates desired total thrust and moments, which are then converted into individual motor commands.

---

# 35. VTOL Pitch Control

Pitch control can similarly be produced through differential thrust between front and rear motor groups.

For example:

$$
T_{front}\neq T_{rear}
$$

The resulting pitching moment is:

$$
M_y=\sum_i r_{x,i}T_i
$$

where \(r_{x,i}\) is the longitudinal distance of each motor from the CG.

---

# 36. VTOL Yaw Control

Yaw control is typically produced by exploiting reaction torques from motors with opposite rotation directions.

A simplified motor torque relationship is:

$$
Q_i\propto\omega_i^2
$$

where:

* \(Q_i\) = motor/propeller reaction torque,
* \(\omega_i\) = rotor angular velocity.

By increasing thrust on one rotation group while decreasing thrust on the opposite group, a net yaw moment can be generated.

The exact relationship must be obtained from the selected motor-propeller system.

---

# 37. Control Allocation

The flight controller ultimately needs to convert desired aircraft forces and moments into actuator commands.

The desired control vector can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{u}_{desired}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
T\\
L\\
M\\
N
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The actuator vector may be:

$$
\mathbf{u}_{act}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
T_1\\
T_2\\
T_3\\
T_4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

For a linearized allocation model:

$$
\mathbf{u}_{desired}
=
B\mathbf{u}_{act}
$$

where \(B\) is the control-allocation matrix.

Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{u}_{act}
=
B^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{desired}
$$

when the matrix is square and invertible.

More generally, a pseudoinverse or constrained optimization method can be used.

---

# 38. Actuator Saturation

The motors have physical limits.

Therefore:

$$
0\leq T_i\leq T_{i,max}
$$

The controller must account for these limits.

If a requested maneuver requires:

$$
T_i>T_{i,max}
$$

the aircraft cannot physically generate the requested control command.

This is particularly important during:

* aggressive transition,
* high-angle maneuvers,
* wind disturbances,
* asymmetric motor operation,
* low battery conditions.

---

# 39. Transition Dynamics

Transition is one of the most important parts of a hybrid VTOL aircraft.

During transition:

* VTOL thrust changes,
* pusher thrust increases,
* wing lift develops,
* aerodynamic drag changes,
* aircraft attitude changes,
* control authority shifts from motor thrust to aerodynamic surfaces.

Therefore, transition is not simply:

```text
VTOL OFF
     ↓
Pusher ON
```

Instead, it is a coupled dynamic process.

---

# 40. Transition Force Balance

A simplified transition model can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{F}_{total}
=
\mathbf{F}_{VTOL}
+
\mathbf{F}_{pusher}
+
\mathbf{F}_{aero}
+
\mathbf{F}_{gravity}
$$

The relative contribution of each force changes with airspeed and aircraft attitude.

At low airspeed:

$$
F_{VTOL}
$$

is dominant.

As airspeed increases:

$$
F_{aero}
$$

becomes increasingly important.

At established forward-flight speed:

$$
L\approx W
$$

and:

$$
T_{pusher}\approx D
$$

become the dominant simplified relationships.

---

# 41. Transition Scheduling

A practical transition controller may use a scheduling variable such as airspeed:

$$
V
$$

or a normalized transition parameter:

$$
\lambda\in[0,1]
$$

where:

$$
\lambda=0
$$

represents VTOL mode and:

$$
\lambda=1
$$

represents forward-flight mode.

The transition schedule may gradually change:

* VTOL motor authority,
* pusher throttle,
* elevator command,
* attitude target,
* control allocation.

The exact transition schedule should be determined through simulation and later testing.

---

# 42. Mode-Based Flight-Dynamics Model

A practical approach is to divide the aircraft into several models.

### Mode 1 — Hover

Dominant effects:

* VTOL thrust,
* gravity,
* rotational dynamics,
* motor torque.

### Mode 2 — Vertical Flight

Dominant effects:

* VTOL thrust,
* gravity,
* vertical velocity,
* attitude.

### Mode 3 — Transition

Dominant effects:

* VTOL thrust,
* pusher thrust,
* aerodynamic forces,
* changing control authority.

### Mode 4 — Forward Flight

Dominant effects:

* wing lift,
* aerodynamic drag,
* pusher thrust,
* control surfaces.

### Mode 5 — Landing

Dominant effects:

* VTOL thrust,
* gravity,
* descent velocity,
* landing loads.

---

# 43. Nonlinear Model

The complete nonlinear aircraft state can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x&
y&
z&
u&
v&
w&
\phi&
\theta&
\psi&
p&
q&
r
\end{bmatrix}^{T}
$$

The control input vector can include:

$$
\mathbf{u}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
T_1&
T_2&
T_3&
T_4&
T_p&
\delta_a&
\delta_e&
\delta_r
\end{bmatrix}^{T}
$$

where:

* \(T_1\ldots T_4\) = VTOL motor thrusts,
* \(T_p\) = rear pusher thrust,
* \(\delta_a\) = aileron deflection,
* \(\delta_e\) = elevator deflection,
* \(\delta_r\) = rudder deflection.

Depending on the actual control architecture, some of these variables may be unavailable or unused in a particular flight mode.

# 44. State-Space Representation

A nonlinear aircraft model can be written as:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})
$$

where:

* \(\mathbf{x}\) = aircraft state,
* \(\mathbf{u}\) = control input,
* \(\mathbf{w}\) = disturbance.

For small perturbations around a trim condition, the model can be linearized:

$$
\Delta\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=
A\Delta\mathbf{x}
+
B\Delta\mathbf{u}
$$

and:

$$
\Delta\mathbf{y}
=
C\Delta\mathbf{x}
+
D\Delta\mathbf{u}
$$

where:

* \(A\) = system matrix,
* \(B\) = input matrix,
* \(C\) = output matrix,
* \(D\) = feedthrough matrix.

* forward velocity,

\mathbf{x}_{lon}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
u&w&q&\theta

or, using angle of attack:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{lon}
=
\begin{bmatrix}

\end{bmatrix}^{T}

Longitudinal analysis is particularly important for:

* cruise,

* pitch stability,

# 47. Hover Dynamics vs Forward-Flight Dynamics

The same control strategy should not automatically be assumed to work equally well in hover and forward flight.

### Hover

Control is primarily produced by:

$$
T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4
$$

### Forward Flight

Control is primarily produced by:

$$
\delta_a,\delta_e,\delta_r
$$

with:

$$
T_p
$$

providing forward propulsion.

### Transition

Both systems contribute:

$$
T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4,T_p,
\delta_a,\delta_e,\delta_r
$$

depending on the transition architecture.

This difference is fundamental to the control design.

Possible disturbances include:

* wind,
* gusts,
* turbulence,
* payload variation,
* motor thrust variation,
* battery voltage variation,
* sensor noise,
* actuator delay,
* aerodynamic-model uncertainty.

A disturbance force can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf{F}_d
$$

and disturbance moment as:

$$
\mathbf{M}_d
$$

Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{F}_{total}
=
\mathbf{F}_{model}
+
\mathbf{F}_d
$$

and:

$$
\mathbf{M}_{total}
=
\mathbf{M}_{model}
+
\mathbf{M}_d
$$

# 49. Wind and Airspeed

Aerodynamic forces depend on air-relative velocity rather than simply ground speed.

Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{V}_{air}
=
\mathbf{V}_{aircraft}
-
\mathbf{V}_{wind}
$$

The magnitude of this relative velocity determines dynamic pressure:

$$
q_\infty
=
\frac12\rho V_{air}^2
$$

This becomes particularly important when evaluating:

* stall,
* cruise,
* gust response,
* transition,
* obstacle avoidance,
* landing.

# 50. Mass Variation

Aircraft mass changes during operation because of:

* battery consumption,
* payload variation,

The flight-dynamics model should therefore not permanently assume one mass value if significant variations are expected.

The preliminary mass is:

* payload changes,

Therefore, different mass configurations should be checked.

# 54. Numerical Integration

The equations of motion are normally integrated numerically.

For a basic time step:

$$
t_{k+1}=t_k+\Delta t
$$

A simple Euler integration can be represented as:

$$
x_{k+1}
=
x_k+\dot{x}_k\Delta t
$$

More accurate methods such as Runge-Kutta integration can be used for nonlinear simulations.

The simulation time step must be sufficiently small to capture the fastest relevant dynamics.

# 56. Stability Analysis

After obtaining a linearized model:

$$
\dot{x}=Ax+Bu
$$

the eigenvalues of \(A\) can be examined.

If:

$$
\lambda_i
$$

are the eigenvalues of the system matrix, their real parts provide information about stability.

For a continuous-time linear system, modes with negative real parts are associated with decaying perturbations.

Modes with positive real parts indicate growing perturbations.

This provides a mathematical method for investigating dynamic stability.

# 57. Dynamic Modes

Fixed-wing aircraft can exhibit dynamic modes such as:

### Short-period mode

Primarily involves:

* angle of attack,
* pitch rate,
* pitch attitude.

### Phugoid mode

Primarily involves:

* airspeed,
* altitude,
* pitch/flight-path oscillation.

### Dutch-roll mode

Coupled:

* yaw,
* sideslip,
* roll.

### Roll subsidence

Primarily roll-rate damping.

### Spiral mode

Slow coupled roll/yaw behavior.

The exact characteristics depend on the aircraft geometry, mass properties, aerodynamic derivatives, and configuration.

# 58. VTOL Dynamic Modes

Hover introduces a different set of dynamics.

Important responses include:

* altitude response,
* roll response,
* pitch response,
* yaw response,
* lateral position response,
* motor thrust response.

The motor/propeller system itself has dynamics.

A simplified actuator model can be:

$$
\tau_m\dot{T}+T=T_{command}
$$

where:

* \(\tau_m\) = motor/actuator time constant,
* \(T\) = actual thrust,
* \(T_{command}\) = commanded thrust.

This prevents the simulation from assuming unrealistically instantaneous motor response.

This distinction between true state and measured state is important for realistic simulation.

# 63. Example Hover Verification

For a hover test:

$$
T_{total}=W
$$

For the preliminary mass:

$$
T_{total}\approx49.05\,N
$$

If the four motors share the load equally:

$$
T_i=\frac{49.05}{4}
$$

$$
\boxed{T_i\approx12.26\,N}
$$

This is the approximate per-motor thrust required for ideal static hover under equal loading.

The previously calculated approximately \(22.1\,N\) per motor corresponds to the preliminary maximum thrust sizing target based on a thrust-to-weight ratio of 1.8, not the thrust required merely to hover.

This distinction should be maintained throughout the project.

# 78. System Identification

Once flight-test data becomes available, unknown model parameters can be estimated.

For example:

* motor thrust coefficients,
* actuator time constants,
* aerodynamic derivatives,
* damping derivatives,
* inertia estimates,
* drag parameters.

A general parameter-estimation problem can be represented as:

$$
\hat{\theta}
=
\arg\min_{\theta}
\sum_k
\left(
y_{measured,k}
-
y_{model,k}(\theta)
\right)^2
$$

where:

* \(\theta\) = model parameters,
* \(y_{measured}\) = measured response,
* \(y_{model}\) = predicted response.

This creates a path from theoretical modeling to experimentally calibrated dynamics.

# 79. Validation Philosophy

A simulation should answer:

> Does the model predict the behavior of the physical aircraft sufficiently well for its intended purpose?

Validation can compare:

$$
y_{simulation}(t)
$$

against:

$$
y_{experiment}(t)
$$

for variables such as:

* altitude,
* pitch,
* roll,
* yaw,
* airspeed,
* climb rate,
* motor output.

The difference can be represented as:

$$
e(t)=y_{experiment}(t)-y_{simulation}(t)
$$

Error metrics can then be calculated.

For example:

$$
RMSE=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
e_i^2
}
$$

The acceptable error should be defined according to the intended use of the model.

# 83. Summary

Flight dynamics converts the aircraft from a collection of designed components into a mathematical representation of a moving system.

The aircraft is modeled as a six-degree-of-freedom rigid body with:

* translational motion,
* rotational motion,
* aerodynamic forces,
* aerodynamic moments,
* four VTOL thrust sources,
* one rear-mounted pusher thrust source,
* control surfaces,
* gravity,
* actuator dynamics,
* environmental disturbances.

The key equations are:

$$
\sum\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}
$$

$$
\sum\mathbf{M}
=
\frac{d\mathbf{H}}{dt}
$$

$$
\mathbf{M}
=
\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}
$$

$$
L=\frac12\rho V^2SC_L
$$

$$
D=\frac12\rho V^2SC_D
$$

$$
M=q_\infty ScC_m
$$

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
$$

The most important engineering principle is that the flight-dynamics model should evolve from preliminary assumptions toward experimentally validated parameters.

The intended progression is:

$$
\boxed{
Design
\rightarrow
Model
\rightarrow
Simulate
\rightarrow
Test
\rightarrow
Compare
\rightarrow
Update
\rightarrow
Validate
}
$$

This establishes the foundation for the next stage of the project: **flight-control system design and implementation**.
