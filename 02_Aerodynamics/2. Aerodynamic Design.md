# 2.2 What Is Aerodynamics?

Aerodynamics is the study of how air moves around objects and how that flow produces forces and moments.

For this UAV, aerodynamics is primarily concerned with:

* lift,
* drag,
* pitching moment,
* airflow behaviour,
* stall,
* wing efficiency,
* airfoil selection,
* wing geometry,
* and interaction between the aircraft and surrounding air.

During forward flight, the main wing is responsible for producing most of the aircraft's lift.

The rear-mounted pusher propulsion system provides forward thrust to overcome aerodynamic drag.

Therefore, the simplified forward-flight force balance is:

$$
L \approx W
$$

and

$$
T_{\mathrm{pusher}} \approx D
$$

where:

* \(L\) = aerodynamic lift,
* \(W\) = aircraft weight,
* \(T_{\mathrm{pusher}}\) = thrust from the rear pusher propulsion system,
* \(D\) = aerodynamic drag.

The four VTOL motors are primarily responsible for vertical flight.

Therefore, the aircraft has two different aerodynamic/propulsive operating conditions:

### VTOL operation

The four lift motors provide the vertical force required to support the aircraft.

### Forward flight

The fixed wing produces aerodynamic lift while the rear pusher motor provides forward thrust.

Transition between these conditions will be studied later.

---

# 2.3 What Is an Airfoil?

An **airfoil** is a two-dimensional cross-sectional shape designed to interact with airflow.

A wing is not simply an airfoil.

The relationship is:

```text
Airfoil
   ↓
Cross-section
   ↓
Wing geometry
   ↓
Three-dimensional aerodynamic surface
```

For example, if the wing uses the same airfoil from root to tip, the airfoil describes the cross-sectional shape while the wing geometry defines:

* span,
* chord,
* taper,
* sweep,
* twist,
* dihedral,
* position,
* and other geometric characteristics.

Therefore:

> **Airfoil selection and wing design are related, but they are not the same problem.**

---

# 2.4 Basic Airfoil Terminology

Before using XFLR5, it is necessary to understand the geometry being analysed.

## 2.4.1 Leading Edge

The **leading edge** is the front-most part of the airfoil relative to the incoming airflow.

For normal forward flight:

```text
          Leading edge
               ↓
             __
          __/  \____
        _/          \__
-------                ------
       ↑
   Trailing edge
```

The leading edge is usually rounded.

Its geometry strongly affects:

* pressure distribution,
* stall behaviour,
* maximum lift,
* and sensitivity to angle of attack.

---

## 2.4.2 Trailing Edge

The **trailing edge** is the rear-most part of the airfoil.

The upper and lower surfaces meet near the trailing edge.

The trailing edge may be:

* mathematically sharp,
* physically truncated,
* or have a finite thickness.

The actual coordinate data must therefore be checked rather than assuming that every airfoil has an exactly zero-thickness trailing edge.

---

# 2.5 Chord Line

The **chord line** is the straight line connecting the leading edge and trailing edge.

The chord length is represented by:

$$
c
$$

For the preliminary aircraft design:

$$
c \approx 0.247\,\mathrm{m}
$$

for the simplified rectangular-wing estimate.

The chord is important because it appears in the Reynolds number:

$$
Re=\frac{\rho Vc}{\mu}
$$

---

# 2.6 Upper and Lower Surfaces

An airfoil has two primary surfaces:

* upper surface,
* lower surface.

The shape of these surfaces controls the pressure distribution around the airfoil.

For a conventional cambered airfoil, the upper surface is generally more curved than the lower surface.

The pressure difference between the surfaces contributes to lift.

---

# 2.7 Camber

**Camber** describes the curvature of the airfoil's mean line relative to the chord line.

A symmetrical airfoil has approximately zero camber.

A cambered airfoil has a curved mean line.

Conceptually:

```text
Symmetrical:

      ______
   __/      \__
----------------
      chord


Cambered:

       ______
    __/      \__
---/------------\---
```

Camber affects the lift characteristics of an airfoil.

A cambered airfoil can generally produce positive lift at a lower or even zero geometric angle of attack compared with a symmetrical airfoil.

---

# 2.8 Airfoil Thickness

Airfoil thickness is the distance between the upper and lower surfaces measured approximately normal to the chord direction.

Thickness is usually expressed as a percentage of chord.

For example, a hypothetical airfoil with maximum thickness equal to \(12\%\) of the chord has:

$$
\frac{t_{\max}}{c}=0.12
$$

For:

$$
c=0.247\,\mathrm{m}
$$

the corresponding maximum thickness would be approximately:

$$
t_{\max}=0.12(0.247)
$$

$$
t_{\max}\approx0.0296\,\mathrm{m}
$$

or approximately:

$$
29.6\,\mathrm{mm}
$$

This does **not** mean that every airfoil named "12%" has exactly the same aerodynamic behaviour.

Thickness distribution and location also matter.

---

# 2.9 Angle of Attack

The **angle of attack**, usually written as

$$
\alpha
$$

is the angle between the airfoil chord line and the relative airflow.

Conceptually:

```text
             Relative airflow
                  ← ← ← ← ←

                    /
                   /  chord line
                  /
                 /
                /
```

The angle between the airflow direction and chord determines the angle of attack.

As \(\alpha\) changes, the aerodynamic forces change.

In general, for a conventional airfoil at small angles of attack:

$$
C_L
$$

increases as:

$$
\alpha
$$

increases.

However, this relationship does not continue indefinitely.

At sufficiently high angle of attack, flow separation can occur and the airfoil may stall.

---

# 2.10 Relative Airflow

The airfoil does not need to physically move through stationary air for aerodynamic analysis.

What matters is the **relative velocity between the air and the airfoil**.

For example, if the UAV flies forward at:

$$
V=15\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

the wing experiences airflow approximately opposite to its forward velocity.

This relative airflow determines the dynamic pressure:

$$
q=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2
$$

where:

* \(q\) = dynamic pressure,
* \(\rho\) = air density,
* \(V\) = relative airspeed.

At:

$$
\rho=1.225\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}
$$

and:

$$
V=15\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

we obtain:

$$
q=\frac{1}{2}(1.225)(15)^2
$$

$$
q\approx137.8\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$

---

# 2.11 Lift

Lift is the aerodynamic force acting approximately perpendicular to the relative airflow.

The standard lift equation is:

$$
L=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_L
$$

or:

$$
L=qSC_L
$$

where:

* \(L\) = lift,
* \(\rho\) = air density,
* \(V\) = airspeed,
* \(S\) = wing reference area,
* \(C_L\) = lift coefficient.

The important observation is that lift depends strongly on velocity.

Because:

$$
L\propto V^2
$$

doubling the airspeed would increase lift by approximately a factor of four if all other quantities remained constant.

---

# 2.12 Lift Coefficient

The **lift coefficient** is a dimensionless quantity describing how effectively a particular aerodynamic geometry produces lift under a given condition.

Rearranging the lift equation gives:

$$
C_L=
\frac{L}
{\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2S}
$$

During steady level flight:

$$
L\approx W
$$

so:

$$
C_L=
\frac{W}
{\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2S}
$$

This equation is useful because it tells us the approximate lift coefficient required by our aircraft at a particular speed.

For the preliminary cruise condition:

$$
W=49.05\,\mathrm{N}
$$

$$
V=15\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

$$
S=0.428\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$

we obtain approximately:

$$
C_L\approx0.83
$$

This value is important because candidate airfoils should be able to operate efficiently around the lift-coefficient range required by the aircraft.

---

# 2.13 Drag

Drag is the aerodynamic force acting approximately opposite to the direction of relative airflow.

The basic drag equation is:

$$
D=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_D
$$

where:

* \(D\) = drag,
* \(C_D\) = drag coefficient.

Like lift coefficient, drag coefficient is dimensionless.

The aircraft's propulsion system must provide sufficient thrust to overcome drag during steady forward flight:

$$
T\approx D
$$

For this project, that thrust is provided primarily by the **rear-mounted pusher propulsion system**.

---

# 2.14 Lift-to-Drag Ratio

The lift-to-drag ratio is:

$$
\frac{L}{D}
$$

It is one of the most useful aerodynamic performance indicators.

Using aerodynamic coefficients:

$$\frac{L}{D}$$
=
$$\frac{C_L}{C_D}$$

A higher \(L/D\) generally means that the aircraft can produce the required lift with less drag under the corresponding condition.

However, the value must always be considered at the relevant:

* Reynolds number,
* angle of attack,
* lift coefficient,
* configuration,
* and flight condition.

Therefore, we should not simply search for the airfoil with the highest advertised \(L/D\).

---

# 2.15 Boundary Layer

The **boundary layer** is the region of airflow close to a solid surface where viscosity strongly affects the flow.

At the aircraft's wing surface, the air velocity relative to the surface approaches approximately zero at the wall due to the no-slip condition.

Moving away from the surface, the velocity increases toward the external-flow velocity.

Conceptually:

```text
External airflow
--------------------------->

        boundary layer
       /---------------
      /
-----/---------------------- wing surface
```

The boundary layer is extremely important because it affects:

* skin-friction drag,
* separation,
* stall,
* transition from laminar to turbulent flow,
* and overall airfoil performance.

---

# 2.16 Laminar and Turbulent Flow

A boundary layer can be approximately:

* laminar,
* transitional,
* turbulent.

### Laminar boundary layer

The flow has a relatively ordered structure.

It can have lower skin-friction drag but may be more susceptible to separation under certain adverse pressure-gradient conditions.

### Turbulent boundary layer

The flow contains stronger mixing and fluctuations.

It generally has higher skin-friction drag but can resist separation better than a laminar boundary layer under many conditions.

For small UAVs, the transition between these regimes can have a significant effect because the Reynolds number is much lower than that of a large transport aircraft.

---

# 2.17 Flow Separation

Flow separation occurs when the boundary layer loses sufficient momentum to remain attached to the surface against the pressure gradient.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Attached flow:

>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>>
---------------------

Separated flow:

>>>>>>>>>>>
        ↗
       ↗
------/------------
```

Separation can cause:

* increased drag,
* reduced lift,
* unstable aerodynamic behaviour,
* and eventually stall.

---

# 2.18 Stall

**Stall is not simply "low speed."**

Stall is primarily associated with the airfoil or wing exceeding its usable angle-of-attack range, causing significant flow separation and loss of lift.

An aircraft can stall at different speeds depending on:

* weight,
* air density,
* wing area,
* configuration,
* load factor,
* flap setting,
* and maximum lift coefficient.

The simplified stall-speed equation is:

$$V_{stall}$$
=
$$\sqrt{
\frac{2W}
{\rho S C_{L_{\max}}}
}$$

Chapter 1 used:

$$
V_{stall}=12\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

and a preliminary:

$$
C_{L_{\max}}=1.3
$$

Chapter 2 will investigate whether candidate airfoils can realistically provide the required lift behaviour.

---

# 2.19 Reynolds Number

Reynolds number is one of the most important parameters in this project.

It is defined as:

$$
Re=
\frac{\rho Vc}{\mu}
$$

where:

* \(\rho\) = air density,
* \(V\) = velocity,
* \(c\) = characteristic length, usually chord for an airfoil,
* \(\mu\) = dynamic viscosity.

It represents the relative importance of inertial effects and viscous effects in the flow.

For our preliminary wing:

$$
c\approx0.247\,\mathrm{m}
$$

At approximately:

$$
V=15\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

the Reynolds number is on the order of:

$$
Re\approx250,000
$$

depending on the exact atmospheric properties used.

This is a **low Reynolds number** regime compared with large conventional aircraft.

This is one of the reasons that airfoil selection must be performed specifically for this UAV.

An airfoil that performs very well at:

$$
Re=3,000,000
$$

cannot automatically be assumed to perform similarly at:

$$
Re=250,000
$$

---

# 2.20 Why Reynolds Number Matters for Airfoil Selection

At lower Reynolds numbers:

* boundary layers behave differently,
* transition can occur differently,
* laminar separation bubbles can become important,
* drag characteristics can change,
* maximum lift can change,
* stall behaviour can change,
* and the optimum airfoil can be different.

Therefore, our evaluation should be performed around the actual operating range.

Instead of analysing only one Reynolds number, we should eventually consider a range such as:

$$
Re=150,000
$$

$$
Re=200,000
$$

$$
Re=250,000
$$

$$
Re=300,000
$$

$$
Re=350,000
$$

The exact final range will be updated when the final wing geometry and operating envelope are known.

---

# 2.21 Airfoil Coordinate Data

An airfoil can be represented numerically using coordinate points.

Each point contains:

$$
(x,y)
$$

where:

* \(x\) represents the position along the chord,
* \(y\) represents the vertical displacement from the reference chord line.

The coordinates are normally normalized by chord:

$$
x/c
$$

and:

$$
y/c
$$

Therefore:

$$
0\leq x/c\leq1
$$

for a conventional airfoil coordinate system.

For example:

```text
x/c       y/c
1.0000    0.0000
0.9500    0.0080
0.9000    0.0150
...
0.0000    0.0000
...
0.9000   -0.0100
0.9500   -0.0050
1.0000    0.0000
```

These points define the airfoil surface.

---

# 2.22 The UIUC Airfoil Coordinates Database

A useful source for obtaining real airfoil coordinate files is the **UIUC Airfoil Coordinates Database** maintained by the University of Illinois.

The database contains approximately 1,650 airfoil coordinate sets and includes airfoils covering applications from low-Reynolds-number aircraft and UAVs to larger aircraft and other applications.

Source:

https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/ads/coord_database.html

The database provides airfoils in `.dat` coordinate format.

The UIUC documentation states that the standard Selig format starts near the upper-surface trailing edge, proceeds around the leading edge, and returns along the lower surface toward the trailing edge.

However, the UIUC site also notes that not every historical airfoil in the database uses exactly the same coordinate ordering convention.

Therefore:

> **Never assume the coordinate ordering just from the filename. Inspect the actual data and plot the airfoil after importing it.**

This is especially important when moving coordinate data between different software packages.

---

# 2.23 What Is a `.dat` Airfoil File?

A `.dat` file is simply a text file containing airfoil information.

A typical file can look conceptually like:

```text
Example Airfoil

1.000000  0.000000
0.950000  0.008000
0.900000  0.014000
...
0.000000  0.000000
...
0.900000 -0.006000
0.950000 -0.003000
1.000000  0.000000
```

The first line normally identifies the airfoil.

The remaining lines contain coordinate pairs.

The coordinates can then be interpreted by aerodynamic software.

---

# 2.25 Why We Should Not Select an Airfoil Immediately

It is tempting to say:

> "This is a UAV, so use airfoil X."

That is not sufficient engineering justification.

Instead, the process should be:

```text
Aircraft requirements
        ↓
Operating Reynolds number
        ↓
Required lift coefficient
        ↓
Candidate airfoils
        ↓
Coordinate data
        ↓
XFLR5 analysis
        ↓
Compare performance
        ↓
Select candidate
```

The selected airfoil should therefore have a documented reason for being selected.

# 2.26 Candidate Airfoil Evaluation Criteria

The candidate airfoils should be compared using several quantities.

## 2.26.1 Lift coefficient

$$
C_L
$$

We need to determine whether the airfoil can provide the required lift coefficient.

Our preliminary cruise requirement is approximately:

$$
C_L\approx0.83
$$

Therefore, the airfoil should have useful performance around this operating region.

---

## 2.26.2 Maximum lift coefficient

$$
C_{L_{\max}}
$$

This is important for stall-speed estimation.

The Chapter 1 value:

$$
C_{L_{\max}}=1.3
$$

is only a preliminary assumption.

XFLR5 analysis can provide an estimate, but the result should not automatically be treated as experimentally validated.

---

## 2.26.3 Drag coefficient

$$
C_D
$$

Lower drag at the required operating condition is generally desirable because it reduces the thrust and power required in forward flight.

---

## 2.26.4 Lift-to-drag ratio

$$
\frac{C_L}{C_D}
$$

This indicates aerodynamic efficiency.

We should compare it at the **same operating condition** between candidate airfoils.

For example:

$$
Re=250,000
$$

and approximately:

$$
C_L=0.8
$$

would be a much more meaningful comparison than comparing unrelated maximum \(L/D\) values.

---

## 2.26.5 Pitching moment

The pitching moment coefficient:

$$
C_m
$$

is important because the airfoil generates not only lift and drag but also aerodynamic moments.

The moment affects:

* aircraft trim,
* tail sizing,
* control authority,
* CG location,
* and stability.

---

# 2.27 Airfoil Polars

An **airfoil polar** is a collection of aerodynamic results obtained over a range of operating conditions.

Common plots include:

### Lift coefficient versus angle of attack

$$
C_L(\alpha)
$$

### Drag coefficient versus angle of attack

$$
C_D(\alpha)
$$

### Lift coefficient versus drag coefficient

$$
C_L(C_D)
$$

### Moment coefficient versus angle of attack

$$
C_m(\alpha)
$$

These plots allow us to understand how the airfoil behaves.

---

# 2.28 The \(C_L-\alpha\) Curve

The \(C_L-\alpha\) curve shows how lift coefficient changes with angle of attack.

A simplified conceptual graph is:

```text
CL
│
│              ______
│            /
│          /
│        /
│      /
│_____/________________ α
│
```

In the approximately linear region:

$$
C_L \approx C_{L0}+a\alpha
$$

where:

* \(C_{L0}\) = lift coefficient at zero angle of attack,
* \(a\) = lift-curve slope,
* \(\alpha\) = angle of attack.

Eventually, the curve becomes nonlinear and the airfoil approaches stall.

---

# 2.29 The Drag Polar

A common simplified aerodynamic model is:

$$
C_D=C_{D0}+kC_L^2
$$

where:

$$
k=\frac{1}{\pi AR e}
$$

For a finite wing, this represents the relationship between parasite/profile drag and induced drag.

The first term:

$$
C_{D0}
$$

represents the zero-lift drag contribution in the simplified model.

The second term:

$$
kC_L^2
$$

represents induced drag.

This equation was used in Chapter 1 as a preliminary estimate.

Chapter 2 begins moving toward more detailed aerodynamic analysis.

---

# 2.30 Airfoil Drag vs Wing Drag

This distinction is extremely important.

A 2-D airfoil analysis produces information about an airfoil section.

A real wing is three-dimensional.

A real wing therefore experiences additional effects such as:

* induced drag,
* finite-span effects,
* tip vortices,
* aspect-ratio effects,
* spanwise lift distribution,
* twist,
* taper,
* dihedral,
* and interactions with the fuselage and other components.

Therefore:

$$
\text{2-D airfoil performance}
\neq
\text{complete aircraft performance}
$$

XFLR5 can be used to progress from airfoil analysis to wing-level analysis, but the assumptions and limitations of the methods must be understood.

---

# 2.31 Aspect Ratio

Aspect ratio is defined as:

$$
AR=\frac{b^2}{S}
$$

where:

* \(b\) = wing span,
* \(S\) = wing area.

For the preliminary design:

$$
AR=7
$$

and:

$$
S=0.428\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$

so:

$$
b=\sqrt{AR\,S}
$$

$$
b=\sqrt{7(0.428)}
$$

$$
b\approx1.73\,\mathrm{m}
$$

A higher aspect ratio generally reduces induced drag for a given lift condition, but it can also affect:

* structural bending loads,
* wing weight,
* packaging,
* roll dynamics,
* manufacturability,
* and VTOL integration.

Therefore, aspect ratio is a design trade-off rather than a number that should simply be maximized.

---

# 2.32 Wing Planform

The wing planform is the shape of the wing viewed from above.

Important parameters include:

* span,
* chord,
* taper ratio,
* sweep,
* dihedral,
* twist,
* wing area.

For the initial design, a simple planform is useful because it reduces unnecessary complexity.

A possible starting point is a rectangular or mildly tapered wing.

The final geometry should be determined after aerodynamic analysis and mechanical integration.

---

# 2.33 Taper Ratio

Taper ratio is:

$$
\lambda=\frac{c_t}{c_r}
$$

where:

* \(c_t\) = tip chord,
* \(c_r\) = root chord.

For a rectangular wing:

$$
\lambda=1
$$

For a tapered wing:

$$
\lambda<1
$$

Taper can influence:

* lift distribution,
* structural efficiency,
* induced drag,
* mass,
* and manufacturing.

---

# 2.34 Wing Twist

Wing twist means that the local airfoil section changes angle along the span.

A common form is **washout**, where the wing tip has a lower local incidence than the root.

Conceptually:

```text
Root                    Tip

Higher incidence  →    Lower incidence
```

Washout can help control the order in which different parts of the wing approach stall.

However, it also changes the spanwise aerodynamic loading.

Therefore, it should be introduced deliberately rather than simply added because it is common on aircraft.

---

# 2.35 Dihedral

Dihedral is the upward angle of the wing relative to the horizontal plane.

It affects lateral-directional behaviour and roll stability.

For the initial aerodynamic analysis, the value can remain a design variable.

The mechanical design must eventually consider the interaction between:

* dihedral,
* VTOL motor placement,
* structural loads,
* landing clearance,
* and CG.

---

# 2.36 What Is XFLR5?

XFLR5 is an aerodynamic analysis tool intended for airfoils, wings, and aircraft operating at low Reynolds numbers.

It incorporates:

* XFoil-based airfoil analysis,
* wing analysis using lifting-line methods,
* Vortex Lattice Method (VLM),
* and a 3-D panel method.

Official website:

https://www.xflr5.com/xflr5.htm

XFLR5 should be considered an **engineering analysis tool**, not an experimental validation system.

Its results depend on:

* input geometry,
* Reynolds number,
* transition assumptions,
* paneling,
* analysis method,
* convergence,
* and the underlying aerodynamic models.

Therefore:

> XFLR5 results should be treated as analytical predictions that help guide the design, not as unquestionable experimental truth.

---

# 2.37 XFLR5 and XFoil

XFLR5 incorporates XFoil-based airfoil analysis.

The simplified relationship is:

```text
Airfoil geometry
       ↓
XFoil-based analysis
       ↓
Airfoil aerodynamic coefficients
       ↓
XFLR5
       ↓
Wing / aircraft analysis
```

XFoil is particularly useful for analysing low-Reynolds-number airfoil behaviour.

XFLR5 adds tools for extending the analysis to finite wings and aircraft configurations.

# 2.38 Important XFLR5 Limitation

XFLR5 documentation explicitly describes limitations and warns that the software is not a professional product with guaranteed robustness or accuracy.

# 2.42 Airfoil Paneling

XFoil-based analysis works with a discretized representation of the airfoil.

The surface is represented using panels.

Too few or poorly distributed panels can reduce the quality of the geometry representation.

The XFLR5 guidelines recommend refinement/paneling of imported or modified foils before analysis.

Therefore, after importing an airfoil:

1. inspect the geometry,
2. refine/re-panel if required,
3. verify the resulting shape,
4. then perform analysis.

Do not modify the geometry without recording what was changed.

# 2.51 Reynolds Number Sweep

One analysis at:

$$
Re=250,000
$$

is not enough.

The UAV will operate over a range of speeds.

Since:

$$
Re=\frac{\rho Vc}{\mu}
$$

Reynolds number changes approximately with airspeed when the other parameters remain similar.

Therefore, analyse multiple Reynolds numbers.

For example:

| Case        | Reynolds number |
| ----------- | --------------: |
| Low         |     \(150,000\) |
| Medium-low  |     \(200,000\) |
| Design      |     \(250,000\) |
| Medium-high |     \(300,000\) |
| High        |     \(350,000\) |

These are initial analysis cases, not final requirements.

---

# 2.52 Why We Need Multiple Reynolds Numbers

Suppose Airfoil A has:

$$
C_D=0.020
$$

at:

$$
Re=250,000
$$

but becomes significantly worse at:

$$
Re=150,000
$$

while Airfoil B has slightly higher drag at \(Re=250,000\) but maintains better behaviour across the full operating range.

The second airfoil may be more suitable depending on the mission.

This is why an engineering comparison should consider the **operating envelope**, not one isolated point.

# 2.57 Finite Wing and Induced Drag

A finite wing creates tip vortices.

The simplified induced-drag relation is:

$$
C_{D_i}
=
\frac{C_L^2}
{\pi AR e}
$$

where:

* \(C_{D_i}\) = induced drag coefficient,
* \(C_L\) = wing lift coefficient,
* \(AR\) = aspect ratio,
* \(e\) = Oswald efficiency factor.

Therefore:

$$
C_{D_i}\propto C_L^2
$$

for the simplified model.

This explains why induced drag becomes particularly important at high-lift conditions such as:

* climb,
* slow flight,
* and approach.

# 2.60 Choosing the Wing Analysis Method

XFLR5 provides several analysis approaches.

Important ones include:

### Lifting Line Theory

Useful for analysing finite wings under assumptions appropriate to lifting-line methods.

### Vortex Lattice Method

VLM represents the lifting surfaces using vortices and can be useful for studying:

* lift distribution,
* induced drag,
* wing loading,
* and effects of planform geometry.

### 3-D Panel Method

Provides a more general surface-based aerodynamic representation.

The choice of method should be documented for every analysis.

Do not compare results from different methods without recording which method produced them.

# 2.63 Airfoil Coordinates and SolidWorks

Once the candidate airfoil has been selected, the coordinate file can be used to create the airfoil profile in CAD.

The general process is:

```text
UIUC .dat file
       ↓
Read x/c and y/c
       ↓
Scale by desired chord
       ↓
Create point coordinates
       ↓
Create spline / curve
       ↓
Create closed airfoil profile
       ↓
Extrude / loft
       ↓
Create wing
```

If the normalized coordinates are:

$$
x/c,\quad y/c
$$

then actual coordinates are:

$$
x=c\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)
$$

and:

$$
y=c\left(\frac{y}{c}\right)
$$

For example, if:

$$
c=0.247\,\mathrm{m}
$$

and:

$$
x/c=0.50
$$

then:

$$
x=0.247(0.50)
$$

$$
x=0.1235\,\mathrm{m}
$$

# 2.64 Important Warning About Coordinate Scaling

The airfoil coordinate data is normally normalized.

Therefore, do not directly interpret:

```text
1.0000
```

as:

$$
1\,\mathrm{m}
$$

It normally means:

$$
x/c=1
$$

which corresponds to the trailing-edge position.

The actual size is determined by the selected chord.

# 2.70 What Counts as a Good Result?

A "good" result is not necessarily the largest number.

For example, if an airfoil produces a very high maximum lift coefficient but has excessive drag at the cruise operating point, that may not satisfy the aircraft's mission.

Likewise, an airfoil with very low drag at one Reynolds number may not behave well over the complete operating range.

Therefore, the selection must consider the aircraft mission.

For this project, the important mission-related questions are:

1. Can the wing generate the required lift?
2. Can the aircraft operate safely around the intended stall speed?
3. Is drag reasonable at cruise?
4. Does the airfoil behave reasonably over the expected Reynolds-number range?
5. Is the pitching moment manageable?
6. Can the airfoil be manufactured accurately?
7. Does the geometry fit the structural requirements?

# 2.74 Limitations of the Analysis

XFLR5 and XFoil are useful engineering tools, but their predictions are subject to modelling assumptions.

Potential sources of error include:

* transition modelling,
* boundary-layer assumptions,
* flow separation prediction,
* low-Reynolds-number effects,
* surface roughness,
* manufacturing imperfections,
* propeller-wing interaction,
* fuselage interference,
* VTOL rotor wake interaction,
* atmospheric variations,
* and three-dimensional flow.

Therefore:

$$
\text{Simulation} \neq \text{Experiment}
$$

Instead:

$$
\text{Simulation}
\rightarrow
\text{Design prediction}
\rightarrow
\text{Prototype}
\rightarrow
\text{Experiment}
\rightarrow
\text{Validation}
$$

given to the range of Reynolds number Re for which each airfoil family has been
designed, considering that the UAV flies at low Reynolds number, more or less
2 ×105, in cruise conditions. The Reynolds number is defined as

$$R_e = \frac{\rho * v * l}{\eta}$$

with $$\rho$$ air density,  v velocity, l characteristic length (in the case of an aircraft it
is the mean aerodynamic chord MAC) and µ kinematic viscosity. This last value
is obtained using the Sutherland formula suggested in [21],

$$\eta = 0.00001716 (\frac{T}{273})^{1.5} \frac{383.7}{T + 110.6}$$

where T is the air temperature in degree Kelvin.
