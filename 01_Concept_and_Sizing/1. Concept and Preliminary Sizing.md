# 1. Concept Design and Preliminary Sizing

## Introduction

At the beginning of a new aircraft project, we usually do not know the final dimensions of the aircraft.

We may know only a few things:

* What the aircraft is supposed to do
* How much payload it should carry
* Whether it needs vertical take-off and landing
* Desired cruise speed
* Approximate endurance
* Type of propulsion
* Operating environment
* Some constraints related to size, weight, cost, or manufacturing

The purpose of **conceptual design** is to convert these initial requirements into a physically meaningful aircraft configuration.

The purpose of **preliminary sizing** is then to estimate the important dimensions and performance parameters of that configuration.

For this project, the design process is:

```text
Mission Requirements
        ↓
Configuration Selection
        ↓
Initial Mass Estimate
        ↓
Wing Loading / Wing Area
        ↓
Wing Geometry
        ↓
Aerodynamic Estimation
        ↓
VTOL Thrust Requirement
        ↓
Forward-Flight Propulsion
        ↓
CG and Component Placement
        ↓
Tail Sizing
        ↓
Battery Sizing
        ↓
Preliminary Structural Sizing
        ↓
Concept CAD
        ↓
Aerodynamic / Structural / Simulation Iteration
```

The important point is that these calculations are **not the final aircraft design**.

They are the first design iteration.

Later, when the aircraft is modeled in CAD, actual component masses, motor locations, battery dimensions, structural masses, aerodynamic results, and propulsion data will change the design.

Therefore, aircraft design is an **iterative process**.

---

# 1.1 What is Conceptual Design?

## Definition

**Conceptual design** is the first stage of aircraft design in which the general architecture and configuration of the aircraft are selected.

At this stage we decide things such as:

* Number of wings
* Wing position
* Tail configuration
* Number and position of motors
* VTOL propulsion arrangement
* Forward-flight propulsion arrangement
* Fuselage arrangement
* Battery location
* Payload location
* General aircraft dimensions

The purpose is not to create a manufacturing-ready CAD model.

Instead, the purpose is to answer:

> **"What type of aircraft should we build to satisfy the mission?"**

For example, a hybrid VTOL UAV can use several architectures:

* Tilt rotor
* Tilt wing
* Tailsitter
* Lift + cruise
* Dual-system propulsion
* Other distributed propulsion configurations

Different architectures have different advantages and disadvantages.

Therefore, configuration selection should happen before detailed CAD.

---

# 1.2 Configuration Selection

For this project, two important configurations were considered:

1. Tilt-rotor configuration
2. Dual-system configuration

---

## 1.2.1 Tilt-Rotor Configuration

<img width="333" height="231" alt="Tilt rotor" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/143d87ea-b472-436b-8254-fe2124c36ef4" />


A **tilt-rotor** aircraft uses motors/rotors whose thrust direction can be rotated.

During vertical flight:

```text
Thrust
  ↑
  |
  |
Aircraft
```

During forward flight, the propulsion system rotates:

```text
Aircraft → → → Forward motion
             ↑
       Thrust direction
```

The same propulsion system therefore performs both vertical and forward-flight propulsion.

### Advantages

* One main propulsion system can perform both flight modes.
* Potentially lower number of motors.
* No separate dedicated forward-flight motor may be required.

### Disadvantages

* Requires a tilting mechanism.
* Mechanical complexity increases.
* Actuators and hinges add weight.
* The transition between vertical and forward flight must be controlled.
* Aerodynamic and control modeling becomes more complicated.
* Failure of the tilt mechanism can become a major system-level problem.

The transition phase is particularly important because the aircraft changes from a condition where rotor thrust supports the aircraft to a condition where the wing provides most of the vertical lift.

# 1.2.2 Dual-System Configuration

The second architecture considered is the **dual-system configuration**.

In this configuration, vertical flight and forward flight use separate propulsion systems.

For example:

```text
             Forward motor
                  →
        __________________
       /                  \
      /        WING        \
     O                    O
    VTOL                VTOL
    motor               motor

              Fuselage
```

The vertical-flight system is responsible mainly for:

* Take-off
* Hover
* Climb
* Descent
* Landing

The forward-flight propulsion system is responsible mainly for:

* Acceleration
* Cruise
* Forward-flight thrust

The wing produces aerodynamic lift during forward flight.

---

## Why is this configuration useful for this project?

One major advantage is that the two flight modes can initially be studied separately.

We can analyze:

```text
Vertical Flight
      ↓
Rotor thrust
      ↓
Hover / climb / descent
```

and:

```text
Forward Flight
      ↓
Wing lift
      ↓
Pusher thrust
      ↓
Cruise
```

This separation makes the first engineering model easier to understand.

However, the configuration has disadvantages.

The aircraft carries two propulsion systems:

* VTOL motors and propellers
* Forward-flight motor and propeller

This adds:

* Mass
* Electrical components
* Drag
* Structural complexity
* Power-management requirements

Therefore, the dual-system configuration is not automatically "better".

It is selected here because it provides a simpler architecture for studying and developing the hybrid VTOL system.

# 1.3 Important Aircraft Design Terms

Before performing calculations, it is important to understand the terminology.

This project uses many terms from aircraft design and aerodynamics.

---

## 1.3.1 UAV

**UAV** means:

> **Unmanned Aerial Vehicle**

It is an aircraft that operates without a human pilot physically sitting inside the aircraft.

The aircraft may be:

* Remotely controlled
* Semi-autonomous
* Fully autonomous

---

## 1.3.2 VTOL

**VTOL** means:

> **Vertical Take-Off and Landing**

A VTOL aircraft can take off and land vertically without requiring a conventional runway.

Examples include:

* Multirotors
* Helicopters
* Tilt-rotors
* Lift + cruise aircraft
* Tailsitters

---

## 1.3.3 Hover

**Hover** means the aircraft is approximately stationary relative to the surrounding air.

For ideal vertical equilibrium:

$$
T = W
$$

where:

* \(T\) = total upward thrust
* \(W\) = aircraft weight

In a real aircraft, additional thrust capability is required for control, wind disturbances, acceleration, and safety margin.

Therefore:

$$
T_{available} > W
$$

---

## 1.3.4 Weight

Weight is a force caused by gravity.

It is calculated as:

$$
W = mg
$$

where:

* \(W\) = weight in Newtons
* \(m\) = mass in kilograms
* \(g\) = gravitational acceleration

For preliminary calculations:

$$
g \approx 9.81\;m/s^2
$$

For a 5 kg aircraft:

$$
W = 5(9.81)
$$

$$
W = 49.05\;N
$$

### Important distinction

Mass and weight are not the same thing.

Mass:

$$
m = 5\;kg
$$

Weight:

$$
W = 49.05\;N
$$

Mass is measured in kilograms.

Weight is measured in Newtons.

This distinction becomes very important in aircraft calculations.

---

# 1.4 Take-Off Weight and Mass Breakdown

## What is MTOW?

**MTOW** means:

> **Maximum Take-Off Weight**

It is the maximum permitted aircraft weight at take-off for the specified design.

For the initial design iteration, we assume:

$$
m_{TO}=5\;kg
$$

Therefore:

$$
W_{TO}=5(9.81)=49.05\;N
$$

This is an **initial design assumption**, not a final validated aircraft weight.

---

# 1.4.1 Initial Mass Breakdown

The initial 5 kg mass budget is divided approximately as follows:

| Subsystem                        | Initial mass |
| -------------------------------- | -----------: |
| Structure, wing and booms        |      1.50 kg |
| Battery                          |      1.40 kg |
| Propulsion and related hardware  |      0.85 kg |
| Avionics, wiring and electronics |      0.30 kg |
| Payload                          |      0.90 kg |
| **Total**                        |  **4.95 kg** |

This leaves approximately:

$$
5.00-4.95=0.05\;kg
$$

of initial margin.

However, this is a very small margin.

Therefore, this table should be treated as a **preliminary mass budget**.

As the project develops, every subsystem should eventually be replaced by an actual estimated or measured mass.

---

# 1.4.2 Why Mass Budget Matters

Aircraft design is strongly coupled to mass.

For example:

```text
Higher battery mass
        ↓
Higher aircraft mass
        ↓
Higher required lift
        ↓
Larger wing / higher wing loading
        ↓
Higher aerodynamic requirements
```

At the same time:

```text
Higher aircraft mass
        ↓
Higher VTOL thrust requirement
        ↓
Larger motors / propellers
        ↓
Higher propulsion mass
        ↓
Even higher aircraft mass
```

This creates a feedback loop.

That is why aircraft sizing cannot be performed by choosing dimensions randomly.

---

# 1.5 Weight Breakdown for an Electric VTOL

For a conventional fuel-powered aircraft, take-off weight can be represented approximately as:

$$
W_{TO}=W_E+W_{fuel}+W_{PL}+W_{crew}
$$

where:

* \(W_{TO}\) = take-off weight
* \(W_E\) = empty aircraft weight
* \(W_{fuel}\) = fuel weight
* \(W_{PL}\) = payload weight
* \(W_{crew}\) = crew weight

For this UAV:

$$
W_{crew}=0
$$

and the aircraft is electrically powered.

Therefore a more appropriate preliminary equation is:

$$
W_{TO}=W_E+W_{batt}+W_{PL}
$$

where:

* \(W_E\) = empty aircraft weight
* \(W_{batt}\) = battery weight
* \(W_{PL}\) = payload weight

This is one of the major differences between conventional fuel-powered aircraft and electric aircraft.

---

# 1.6 Why the Fuel-Fraction Method Does Not Directly Apply

In a conventional aircraft, fuel is consumed during flight.

Therefore:

$$
m_{fuel,final}<m_{fuel,initial}
$$

and aircraft mass decreases during the mission.

A battery behaves differently.

If a LiPo battery provides electrical energy to the motors, the chemical energy stored inside the battery decreases, but its physical mass remains approximately constant during the flight.

Therefore, for preliminary sizing:

> Battery energy consumption does not produce the same aircraft-mass reduction as fuel consumption.

The battery must therefore be considered primarily through:

* Energy capacity
* Maximum power capability
* Voltage
* Current
* Discharge rate
* Thermal limits
* Mass

This becomes important later during battery sizing.

# 1.7 Preliminary Sizing

## What is Preliminary Sizing?

Preliminary sizing means estimating the major aircraft parameters before detailed CAD.

The most important initial parameters include:

* Take-off mass
* Wing area
* Wing span
* Mean chord
* Aspect ratio
* Stall speed
* Cruise speed
* VTOL thrust
* Propeller diameter
* Cruise drag
* Cruise power
* Battery capacity
* CG location
* Tail area

The goal is not to obtain exact final values.

The goal is to obtain a **reasonable first design point**.

---

# 1.8 Wing Loading

## What is Wing Loading?

Wing loading describes how much aircraft weight is carried by a given wing area.

It is defined as:

$$
\frac{W}{S}
$$

where:

* \(W\) = aircraft weight
* \(S\) = wing planform area

Units are:

$$
N/m^2
$$

Wing loading is an important aircraft design parameter.

### Low wing loading

Generally means:

* Lower stall speed
* Larger wing
* Better low-speed capability
* Potentially better take-off and landing performance

But the larger wing can increase:

* Structural mass
* Area
* Drag
* Packaging difficulty

### High wing loading

Generally means:

* Smaller wing
* Higher stall speed
* Potentially more compact aircraft
* Higher low-speed requirements

Therefore wing loading is a design trade-off.

---

# 1.9 Aerodynamic Fundamentals

Before sizing the wing, we need to understand the four major aerodynamic forces.

An aircraft in forward flight is mainly affected by:

```text
                Lift
                 ↑
                 |
Drag  ←      Aircraft      → Thrust
                 |
                 ↓
               Weight
```

These four forces are:

### Lift

Lift acts approximately perpendicular to the relative airflow.

### Drag

Drag acts approximately opposite to the aircraft's direction of motion through the air.

### Thrust

Thrust is generated by the propulsion system.

### Weight

Weight acts downward due to gravity.

---

# 1.9.1 Lift

Lift is the aerodynamic force generated by the wing.

The basic lift equation is:

$$
L=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_L
$$

where:

* \(L\) = lift force
* \(\rho\) = air density
* \(V\) = aircraft airspeed
* \(S\) = wing area
* \(C_L\) = lift coefficient

The equation tells us something very important:

Lift depends on:

* Air density
* Airspeed
* Wing area
* Aerodynamic efficiency/configuration

---

# 1.9.2 Dynamic Pressure

The term:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2
$$

appears repeatedly in aerodynamics.

It is called **dynamic pressure**.

We define it as:

$$
q=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2
$$

Therefore lift can also be written:

$$
L=qSC_L
$$

Dynamic pressure represents the pressure scale associated with the aircraft's motion through the air.

Notice that velocity is squared.

Therefore, increasing airspeed has a strong effect on aerodynamic forces.

For example:

If velocity doubles:

$$
V_2=2V_1
$$

then:

$$
V_2^2=4V_1^2
$$

Therefore, all else being equal, the aerodynamic force scale becomes approximately four times larger.

---

# 1.9.3 Lift Coefficient

The **lift coefficient**, \(C_L\), is a dimensionless number describing how effectively the aircraft configuration produces lift under a given aerodynamic condition.

It depends on factors such as:

* Airfoil shape
* Angle of attack
* Reynolds number
* Mach number
* Wing geometry
* Flaps or control surfaces
* Surface condition

The lift coefficient is not a constant for all flight conditions.

As angle of attack increases, \(C_L\) generally increases until the wing approaches stall.

---

# 1.9.4 Maximum Lift Coefficient

The maximum lift coefficient is written as:

$$
C_{L_{\max}}
$$

It represents the approximate maximum lift coefficient available before significant flow separation and stall occur.

It depends strongly on:

* Airfoil
* Reynolds number
* Surface condition
* Wing geometry
* Angle of attack
* Flaps
* Three-dimensional wing effects

Therefore \(C_{L_{\max}}\) should not simply be treated as a universal constant.

For this preliminary design, an initial value of:

$$
C_{L_{\max}}\approx1.3
$$

is assumed.

This value must later be checked using aerodynamic analysis.

---

# 1.10 Stall Speed

## What is Stall?

A **stall** occurs when the wing exceeds its usable angle of attack and can no longer maintain the required lift efficiently.

Stall does **not** simply mean that the aircraft's engine stops.

An aircraft can stall even when the motor is producing thrust.

The important aerodynamic condition is excessive angle of attack.

---

# 1.10.1 Stall Speed Equation

At stall, the wing is approximately operating at its maximum usable lift coefficient:

$$
L=W
$$

Using the lift equation:

$$
L=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_L
$$

At stall:

$$
W=\frac{1}{2}\rho V_{stall}^2SC_{L_{\max}}
$$

Rearranging:

$$
V_{stall}$$
=
$$\sqrt{
\frac{2W}
{\rho SC_{L_{\max}}}
}
$$

This equation is extremely important for preliminary wing sizing.

---

# 1.10.2 Rearranging for Wing Area

If we want to determine the required wing area:

$$
S=
\frac{2W}
{\rho V_{stall}^2C_{L_{\max}}}
$$

This is the equation we will use for the first wing sizing calculation.

---

# 1.10.3 Meaning of Each Term

### \(S\)

Wing planform area.

Unit:

$$
m^2
$$

### \(W\)

Aircraft weight.

Unit:

$$
N
$$

Calculated using:

$$
W=mg
$$

### \(\rho\)

Air density.

For standard sea-level conditions, a preliminary value is:

$$
\rho=1.225\;kg/m^3
$$

Actual air density changes with:

* Altitude
* Temperature
* Pressure

### \(V_{stall}\)

Stall speed in forward flight.

Unit:

$$
m/s
$$

### \(C_{L_{\max}}\)

Maximum lift coefficient.

Dimensionless.

---

# 1.11 Selecting an Initial Stall Speed

The stall speed should not be chosen randomly.

A lower stall speed generally requires a larger wing area.

A higher stall speed allows a smaller wing but increases low-speed operating requirements.

For this initial concept, we select:

$$
V_{cruise}=15\;m/s
$$

and initially assume:

$$
V_{stall}=12\;m/s
$$

This gives:

$$
\frac{V_{cruise}}{V_{stall}}
=
\frac{15}{12}
=
1.25
$$

This means the initial cruise speed is approximately 25% higher than the assumed stall speed.

This is only a preliminary design choice.

Later, the actual stall speed will be checked after selecting and analyzing the airfoil and wing.

---

# 1.12 Initial Wing Area Calculation

Assume:

$$
m=5\;kg
$$

Therefore:

$$
W=mg
$$

$$
W=5(9.81)
$$

$$
W=49.05\;N
$$

Assume:

$$
\rho=1.225\;kg/m^3
$$

$$
V_{stall}=12\;m/s
$$

$$
C_{L_{\max}}=1.3
$$

Using:

$$
S=
\frac{2W}
{\rho V_{stall}^2C_{L_{\max}}}
$$

Substitute:

$$
S=
\frac{2(49.05)}
{1.225(12)^2(1.3)}
$$

$$
S=
\frac{98.10}
{1.225(144)(1.3)}
$$

$$
S\approx0.428\;m^2
$$

Therefore:

> **Initial wing area:**

$$
\boxed{S\approx0.428\;m^2}
$$

This is a preliminary sizing result.

It is not yet the final wing area.

# 1.13 What is Aspect Ratio?

**Aspect ratio**, usually written as \(AR\), describes how long and slender a wing is relative to its area.

For a simple wing:

$$
AR=\frac{b^2}{S}
$$

where:

* \(AR\) = aspect ratio
* \(b\) = wingspan
* \(S\) = wing area

A higher aspect ratio generally means a longer, narrower wing.

A lower aspect ratio generally means a shorter, wider wing.

---

# 1.13.1 Why Aspect Ratio Matters

Aspect ratio strongly affects induced drag.

A higher aspect ratio generally reduces induced drag for a given lift condition.

This can improve cruise efficiency.

However, increasing aspect ratio also creates challenges:

* Longer wing
* Greater bending moment
* Greater structural requirements
* Potentially greater structural mass
* More difficult transportation and manufacturing

Therefore, the highest possible aspect ratio is not automatically the best choice.

---

# 1.13.2 Initial Aspect Ratio Selection

For this aircraft, an initial value is selected:

$$
AR=7
$$

This is a preliminary compromise between:

* Cruise efficiency
* Structural stiffness
* Aircraft size
* VTOL integration
* Manufacturing practicality

The value will later be refined.

---

# 1.14 Wing Span

Starting from:

$$
AR=\frac{b^2}{S}
$$

Rearrange:

$$
b=\sqrt{AR\cdot S}
$$

Using:

$$
AR=7
$$

and:

$$
S=0.428\;m^2
$$

we obtain:

$$
b=\sqrt{7(0.428)}
$$

$$
b\approx1.73\;m
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{b\approx1.73\;m}
$$

This is the initial wingspan.

---

# 1.15 What is Chord?

The **chord** of an airfoil is the straight-line distance from the leading edge to the trailing edge.

```text
Leading Edge
     ●────────────────────●
                          Trailing Edge
          Chord
```

For a simple rectangular wing:

$$
S=b\,c
$$

Therefore:

$$
c=\frac{S}{b}
$$

Using:

$$
S=0.428\;m^2
$$

and:

$$
b=1.73\;m
$$

we obtain:

$$
c=\frac{0.428}{1.73}
$$

$$
c\approx0.247\;m
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{c\approx0.247\;m}
$$

or approximately:

$$
\boxed{c\approx25\;cm}
$$

This is the initial mean chord for the simplified rectangular-wing calculation.

---

# 1.16 What is Mean Aerodynamic Chord?

The **Mean Aerodynamic Chord**, or **MAC**, is a reference chord used to describe the aerodynamic behavior and CG location of a wing.

For a simple rectangular wing:

$$
MAC=c
$$

Therefore, in our preliminary design:

$$
MAC\approx0.247\;m
$$

For a tapered wing, MAC is not simply equal to the root chord.

The exact MAC must be calculated from the wing geometry.

This will become important when:

* Calculating CG location
* Calculating static margin
* Positioning the aerodynamic center
* Sizing the tail

# 1.17 Airfoil

## What is an Airfoil?

An **airfoil** is the cross-sectional shape of a wing or aerodynamic surface.

A wing is essentially an airfoil extended across a span with a particular planform.

An airfoil has several important geometric features:

* Leading edge
* Trailing edge
* Chord line
* Camber
* Thickness
* Upper surface
* Lower surface

---

# 1.17.1 Leading Edge

The **leading edge** is the front part of the airfoil that first encounters the airflow.

---

# 1.17.2 Trailing Edge

The **trailing edge** is the rear edge where the upper and lower surfaces meet.

---

# 1.17.3 Camber

**Camber** describes the curvature of the airfoil.

A cambered airfoil is not symmetric about the chord line.

Camber can allow an airfoil to generate positive lift at relatively low angles of attack.

---

# 1.17.4 Symmetric Airfoil

A symmetric airfoil has approximately identical upper and lower geometry around the chord line.

Symmetric airfoils are useful for some applications, particularly where similar aerodynamic behavior is desired in positive and negative lift conditions.

However, for a conventional fixed-wing cruise UAV, a cambered airfoil may be more suitable for efficient positive lift.

---

# 1.18 Reynolds Number

After determining approximate wing dimensions and flight speed, we can estimate the Reynolds number.

This is extremely important for small UAVs.

---

## What is Reynolds Number?

The **Reynolds number**, written as \(Re\), is a dimensionless parameter that describes the relative importance of inertial effects and viscous effects in fluid flow.

For an airfoil:

$$
Re=\frac{\rho Vc}{\mu}
$$

where:

* \(Re\) = Reynolds number
* \(\rho\) = air density
* \(V\) = velocity
* \(c\) = characteristic length, usually airfoil chord
* \(\mu\) = dynamic viscosity of air

At approximately standard conditions:

$$
\mu\approx1.81\times10^{-5}\;Pa\cdot s
$$

---

# 1.18.1 Why Reynolds Number Matters

The same airfoil does not necessarily behave the same way at every size and speed.

For example:

```text
Large aircraft
      ↓
Large chord + high speed
      ↓
Higher Reynolds number
```

while:

```text
Small UAV
      ↓
Small chord + lower speed
      ↓
Lower Reynolds number
```

At low Reynolds numbers:

* Boundary-layer behavior changes
* Flow separation can become more important
* Laminar and turbulent transition behavior changes
* Maximum lift can change
* Drag can change
* Airfoil selection becomes more sensitive

Therefore, an airfoil that performs well on a large aircraft may not perform equally well on a small UAV.

---

# 1.18.2 Reynolds Number Calculation

Using:

$$
\rho=1.225\;kg/m^3
$$

$$
V=15\;m/s
$$

$$
c=0.247\;m
$$

$$
\mu=1.81\times10^{-5}\;Pa\cdot s
$$

we obtain:

$$
Re=
\frac{1.225(15)(0.247)}
{1.81\times10^{-5}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
Re\approx2.5\times10^5
$$

or approximately:

$$
\boxed{Re\approx250,000}
$$

This places the aircraft in a relatively low-Reynolds-number regime compared with larger aircraft.

This is one of the reasons why the airfoil must be analyzed at the actual Reynolds number relevant to this aircraft.

---

# 1.19 Airfoil Selection

Possible initial airfoil candidates include:

* Clark Y
* NACA 4412
* SD7037
* Eppler-type low-Reynolds-number airfoils

However:

> An airfoil should not be selected only because its name is commonly used on UAVs.

The airfoil should be compared using:

* Reynolds number
* \(C_L\)
* \(C_D\)
* \(C_L/C_D\)
* \(C_{L_{\max}}\)
* Stall behavior
* Angle of attack
* Surface condition

This is where **XFLR5/XFOIL-based analysis** becomes useful.

The airfoil selection will therefore be treated as an engineering comparison rather than an arbitrary choice.

# 1.20 Airfoil Coordinates

An airfoil can be represented numerically using coordinate points.

A typical coordinate file contains:

$$
x,\;y
$$

where:

* \(x\) = position along the chord
* \(y\) = vertical position relative to the chord

Usually the chord is normalized:

$$
c=1
$$

so:

$$
0\leq x\leq1
$$

For example:

```text
x       y
1.000   0.000
0.900   ...
0.800   ...
...
0.000   0.000
...
```

These coordinates can be imported into aerodynamic software and later used to create the airfoil geometry in CAD.

This gives us a useful engineering chain:

```text
Airfoil coordinates
        ↓
XFOIL / XFLR5
        ↓
Aerodynamic coefficients
        ↓
Airfoil selection
        ↓
CAD spline
        ↓
Wing geometry
```

# 1.21 VTOL Lift Rotor Sizing

The aircraft uses four dedicated VTOL motors.

Their purpose is to generate vertical thrust during:

* Take-off
* Hover
* Climb
* Descent
* Landing
* Potential transition assistance

The first step is to estimate the total required thrust.

---

# 1.21.1 Thrust-to-Weight Ratio

For hovering:

$$
T=W
$$

But an aircraft should not be designed so that its maximum available thrust is only equal to its weight.

We need additional thrust for:

* Control
* Wind
* Acceleration
* Disturbances
* Climb
* Transition
* Safety margin

Therefore we define a preliminary thrust-to-weight ratio:

$$
\frac{T_{total}}{W}=k
$$

or:

$$
T_{total}=kW
$$

For the initial calculation, assume:

$$
k=1.8
$$

---

# 1.21.2 Total VTOL Thrust

Aircraft weight:

$$
W=49.05\;N
$$

Therefore:

$$
T_{total}=1.8(49.05)
$$

$$
T_{total}\approx88.3\;N
$$

For four motors:

$$
T_{motor}=\frac{T_{total}}{4}
$$

$$
T_{motor}=\frac{88.3}{4}
$$

$$
T_{motor}\approx22.1\;N
$$

Therefore the initial target is:

$$
\boxed{T_{motor}\approx22.1\;N}
$$

This is a preliminary sizing target.

The actual motor and propeller combination must later be selected using manufacturer thrust data or experimental thrust measurements.

---

# 1.22 Propeller Disk Area

A propeller produces thrust by accelerating air.

A simplified momentum-theory model considers the area swept by the propeller.

For a circular propeller:

$$
A=\frac{\pi D^2}{4}
$$

where:

* \(A\) = propeller disk area
* \(D\) = propeller diameter

---

# 1.22.1 Disk Loading

Disk loading is:

$$
DL=\frac{T}{A}
$$

where:

* \(DL\) = disk loading
* \(T\) = thrust
* \(A\) = rotor disk area

Rearranging:

$$
A=\frac{T}{DL}
$$

and:

$$
D=
\sqrt{\frac{4T}{\pi DL}}
$$

This shows why propeller diameter cannot be selected from thrust alone.

We also need to choose an acceptable disk loading.

Generally, larger rotor disk area can reduce the induced velocity required to generate a given thrust and can improve hover efficiency, subject to practical constraints.

Therefore the final propeller diameter will be selected after:

* Motor selection
* Propeller selection
* Frame geometry
* Propeller clearance
* Structural constraints
* Noise requirements
* Available battery power

are considered together.

---

# 1.23 Hover Power

A simplified momentum-theory expression for ideal hover power is:

$$
P_{ideal}
=
\frac{T^{3/2}}
{\sqrt{2\rho A}}
$$

where:

* \(P_{ideal}\) = ideal induced power
* \(T\) = thrust
* \(\rho\) = air density
* \(A\) = total rotor disk area

This equation assumes idealized momentum-theory behavior.

The real system will require more power because of:

* Rotor losses
* Profile drag
* Motor losses
* ESC losses
* Propeller inefficiency
* Non-uniform inflow
* Rotor-rotor interference

Therefore:

$$
P_{actual}
=
\frac{P_{ideal}}{FM}
$$

where \(FM\) is the **Figure of Merit**.

---

# 1.23.1 What is Figure of Merit?

The **Figure of Merit**, abbreviated as \(FM\), is a measure of rotor efficiency in hover.

It compares ideal rotor performance with actual rotor performance.

A higher \(FM\) means the rotor is operating closer to the ideal momentum-theory performance.

The exact value depends on:

* Rotor geometry
* Blade design
* Reynolds number
* Operating condition
* Rotor losses

Therefore an assumed \(FM\) should eventually be replaced by manufacturer data or experimentally validated data.

---

# 1.24 Forward-Flight Aerodynamics

The VTOL motors are mainly responsible for vertical flight.

During forward flight, the wing generates most of the lift.

The pusher motor generates the forward thrust required to overcome drag.

The fundamental equilibrium is:

$$
L\approx W
$$

and:

$$
T\approx D
$$

where:

* \(L\) = lift
* \(W\) = weight
* \(T\) = thrust
* \(D\) = drag

---

# 1.25 Lift Coefficient at Cruise

From:

$$
L=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_L
$$

and:

$$
L=W
$$

we obtain:

$$
C_L=
\frac{2W}
{\rho V^2S}
$$

For:

$$
W=49.05\;N
$$

$$
\rho=1.225\;kg/m^3
$$

$$
V=15\;m/s
$$

$$
S=0.428\;m^2
$$

we obtain:

$$
C_L=
\frac{2(49.05)}
{1.225(15)^2(0.428)}
$$

$$
C_L\approx0.83
$$

Therefore the preliminary cruise lift coefficient is:

$$
\boxed{C_L\approx0.83}
$$

This value is useful when estimating drag.

# 1.26 Drag

## What is Drag?

**Drag** is the aerodynamic force that opposes the aircraft's motion through the air.

Drag can be divided into several components.

Important components include:

* Parasite drag
* Induced drag
* Skin-friction drag
* Form drag
* Interference drag
* Wave drag at higher Mach numbers

For this low-speed UAV, wave drag is not a primary concern.

A simplified drag model is:

$$
C_D=C_{D0}+C_{D_i}
$$

where:

* \(C_D\) = total drag coefficient
* \(C_{D0}\) = zero-lift / parasite drag component
* \(C_{D_i}\) = induced drag component

---

# 1.26.1 Induced Drag

A common preliminary model for induced drag is:

$$
C_{D_i}
=
\frac{C_L^2}
{\pi AR e}
$$

Therefore:

$$
C_D
=
C_{D0}
+
\frac{C_L^2}
{\pi AR e}
$$

where:

* \(C_{D0}\) = parasite drag coefficient
* \(C_L\) = lift coefficient
* \(AR\) = aspect ratio
* \(e\) = Oswald efficiency factor

---

# 1.26.2 What is Oswald Efficiency?

The **Oswald efficiency factor**, \(e\), represents how efficiently the finite wing produces lift compared with an idealized reference.

It accounts approximately for effects such as:

* Lift distribution
* Wing planform
* Tip effects
* Three-dimensional aerodynamic behavior

For preliminary calculations, a value around:

$$
e\approx0.75-0.8
$$

may be used.

However, the final value should come from more detailed aerodynamic analysis.

---

Assume:

$$
C_{D0}=0.03
$$

$$
C_L=0.831
$$

$$
AR=7
$$

$$
e=0.78
$$

Then:

$$
C_D
=
0.03+
\frac{0.831^2}
{\pi(7)(0.78)}
$$

$$
C_D\approx0.070
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{C_D\approx0.070}
$$

This is a first-order estimate only.

The actual aircraft drag coefficient should later be obtained using:

* XFLR5
* CFD where appropriate
* Component drag estimates
* Experimental testing

# 1.29 Cruise Power

The aerodynamic power required to overcome drag is:

$$
P_{aero}=DV
$$

Using:

$$
D=4.15\;N
$$

and:

$$
V=15\;m/s
$$

we obtain:

$$
P_{aero}=4.15(15)
$$

$$
P_{aero}\approx62.3\;W
$$

However, the motor must provide more electrical power than this because the propulsion system is not 100% efficient.

If the propulsive efficiency is:

$$
\eta_{prop}=0.70
$$

then:

$$
P_{motor}
=
\frac{DV}{\eta_{prop}}
$$

$$
P_{motor}
=
\frac{4.15(15)}{0.70}
$$

$$
P_{motor}\approx89\;W
$$

Therefore the preliminary cruise propulsion power is:

$$
\boxed{P_{cruise}\approx89\;W}
$$

This value is surprisingly small compared with the power required during VTOL operation.

That is an important characteristic of hybrid VTOL aircraft:

> Forward flight can be much more energy-efficient than sustained hover because the wing produces lift aerodynamically instead of requiring the rotors to continuously support the full aircraft weight.

---

# 1.30 Why Cruise Power and Hover Power Are Very Different

During hover:

```text
Rotor
 ↓
Accelerates a large amount of air downward
 ↓
Generates vertical thrust
```

During forward flight:

```text
Aircraft moves forward
 ↓
Wing generates lift
 ↓
Propeller mainly overcomes drag
```

Therefore the power requirement can change significantly between the two modes.

This is one of the fundamental reasons for using a hybrid VTOL architecture.

---

# 1.31 Pusher Motor Sizing

The pusher motor must not be selected only from the calculated cruise power.

It must also be checked for:

* Static thrust
* Acceleration
* Climb
* Transition
* Propeller efficiency
* Continuous power
* Peak power
* Battery current

A common mistake would be:

> "Cruise requires only 89 W, therefore select a motor rated at 89 W."

That would be unsafe as a design method.

The motor must have sufficient margin above the predicted continuous cruise requirement.

The static thrust requirement is especially important during transition.

---

# 1.32 Transition Consideration

During the transition from VTOL to forward flight, the aircraft changes from:

```text
Rotor-supported flight
        ↓
Wing-supported flight
```

The aircraft must accelerate sufficiently so that the wing generates enough lift.

A simplified relationship is:

$$
L=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_L
$$

As velocity increases, lift capability increases approximately with:

$$
V^2
$$

Therefore the aircraft needs sufficient forward acceleration before the vertical lift system is reduced significantly.

This makes transition one of the most important parts of a hybrid VTOL design.

Later chapters will model this more carefully.

---

# 1.33 Fuselage / Body Sizing

The fuselage is not sized only by aerodynamic appearance.

It must contain and support:

* Battery
* Flight controller
* ESCs
* Wiring
* Communication systems
* Sensors
* Payload
* Forward motor system
* Structural connections

The battery is particularly important because it can be one of the heaviest individual components.

Therefore the fuselage should be designed around a **component packaging study**.

---

# 1.34 What is Centre of Gravity?

The **Centre of Gravity**, abbreviated as **CG**, is the point at which the total weight of the aircraft can be considered to act.

For a stable aircraft design, CG location is extremely important.

Changing the location of a heavy component changes the CG.

For example:

```text
Battery moved forward
        ↓
CG moves forward
```

and:

```text
Battery moved backward
        ↓
CG moves backward
```

Therefore CAD and mass properties will become an important part of CG determination.

---

# 1.35 Longitudinal CG Calculation

For discrete components, the longitudinal CG can be estimated using:

$$
x_{CG}
=
\frac{\sum m_ix_i}
{\sum m_i}
$$

where:

* \(m_i\) = mass of component \(i\)
* \(x_i\) = longitudinal position of component \(i\)
* \(x_{CG}\) = longitudinal CG position

For example:

| Component |    Mass | Position |
| --------- | ------: | -------: |
| Battery   | \(m_1\) |  \(x_1\) |
| Motor     | \(m_2\) |  \(x_2\) |
| Payload   | \(m_3\) |  \(x_3\) |
| Avionics  | \(m_4\) |  \(x_4\) |
| Structure | \(m_5\) |  \(x_5\) |

Then:

$$
x_{CG}
=
\frac{
m_1x_1+m_2x_2+m_3x_3+\cdots
}
{
m_1+m_2+m_3+\cdots
}
$$

This calculation will eventually be performed directly from the CAD mass properties.

---

# 1.36 CG and Mean Aerodynamic Chord

For a conventional fixed-wing aircraft, CG position is often expressed as a percentage of the Mean Aerodynamic Chord:

$$
\%MAC
=
\frac{x_{CG}-x_{LE,MAC}}
{MAC}
\times100
$$

where:

* \(x_{CG}\) = CG position
* \(x_{LE,MAC}\) = leading-edge position of the MAC
* \(MAC\) = mean aerodynamic chord

An initial CG target may be placed around:

$$
25\%-30\%
$$

of MAC for preliminary fixed-wing sizing.

However, this is **not a universal stability requirement**.

The final CG location must be determined from:

* Aerodynamic center
* Static margin
* Tail volume
* Aircraft configuration
* Control authority
* Flight dynamics

Therefore the 25–30% value is used only as an initial design reference.

---

# 1.37 What is Aerodynamic Center?

The **Aerodynamic Center**, or **AC**, is a point along the airfoil/wing where the pitching moment is approximately independent of angle of attack over the applicable linear range.

The relationship between:

```text
CG
and
Aerodynamic Center
```

is important for aircraft stability.

The distance between them influences the aircraft's **static margin**.

---

# 1.38 What is Static Margin?

Static margin is a measure of longitudinal static stability.

A simplified definition is:

$$
SM=
\frac{x_{NP}-x_{CG}}
{MAC}
$$

where:

* \(x_{NP}\) = neutral point
* \(x_{CG}\) = centre of gravity
* \(MAC\) = mean aerodynamic chord

A positive static margin generally indicates that the CG is ahead of the neutral point.

The exact stability analysis will be performed later.

Therefore, at this stage:

> CG position is a preliminary target, not a final validated stability result.

---

# 1.39 VTOL Boom / Arm Sizing

The four VTOL motors require structural support.

The motor arms or booms must carry:

* Motor mass
* Propeller loads
* Thrust reaction
* Bending loads
* Vibration
* Landing loads
* Potential asymmetric loads

The arm should therefore not be selected only by visual appearance.

---

# 1.39.1 Propeller Clearance

Adjacent propellers should have sufficient separation.

A preliminary spacing rule may be considered:

$$
d_{motor}\gtrsim1.2-1.5D
$$

where:

* \(d_{motor}\) = distance between motor centers
* \(D\) = propeller diameter

This is only a preliminary geometric guideline.

The final spacing should consider:

* Rotor wake interaction
* Structural geometry
* Propeller diameter
* Motor size
* Control requirements
* Vibration
* Packaging

---

# 1.39.2 Symmetry

The four VTOL motors should initially be positioned symmetrically around the aircraft CG.

For example:

```text
        Front

      O       O
      |       |
      |  CG   |
      |       |
      O       O

        Rear
```

Symmetry helps distribute the thrust forces.

If the geometry is highly asymmetric, the flight controller may need continuous corrective commands to maintain attitude.

This can increase:

* Control effort
* Power consumption
* Motor loading

Therefore the first CAD layout should aim for a balanced VTOL geometry.

# 1.40 Tail Configuration

The tail is part of the aircraft's **empennage**.

The empennage provides important aerodynamic functions such as:

* Pitch control
* Yaw control
* Longitudinal stability
* Directional stability

Several tail configurations can be considered.

---

## 1.40.1 Conventional Tail

A conventional tail has:

* Horizontal stabilizer
* Vertical stabilizer

```text
          Main Wing
       ______________
             |
             |
             |
             |
             |
             |
             |
             |
            ───
         Horizontal
          stabilizer
              |
              |
          Vertical
           fin
```

It is relatively simple and widely used.

---

# 1.40.2 T-Tail

In a T-tail:

```text
       ─────────
       Horizontal
        stabilizer
            |
            |
            |
         Vertical
           fin
```

The horizontal stabilizer is mounted near the top of the vertical stabilizer.

Advantages can include cleaner airflow in some configurations.

Disadvantages include:

* Additional structural weight
* Structural complexity
* Potential deep-stall considerations

---

# 1.40.3 V-Tail

A V-tail uses two surfaces arranged at an angle.

The surfaces perform combined:

* Pitch
* Yaw

functions.

This requires control mixing.

---

# 1.40.4 Twin Tail

A twin-tail configuration uses two vertical stabilizers.

It can provide packaging and directional-control advantages depending on the aircraft layout.

# 1.41 Horizontal Tail Sizing

A common preliminary method is the **horizontal tail volume coefficient**.

It is defined as:

$$
V_H=
\frac{S_{HT}L_{HT}}
{S_{wing}MAC}
$$

where:

* \(V_H\) = horizontal tail volume coefficient
* \(S_{HT}\) = horizontal tail area
* \(L_{HT}\) = horizontal tail moment arm
* \(S_{wing}\) = main wing area
* \(MAC\) = mean aerodynamic chord

Rearranging:

$$
S_{HT}
=
\frac{
V_HS_{wing}MAC
}
{L_{HT}}
$$

---

# 1.41.1 What is Tail Volume Coefficient?

The tail volume coefficient is a nondimensional measure of the size and effectiveness of the tail relative to the main wing.

It combines:

* Tail area
* Tail arm
* Main wing area
* Main wing chord

A larger tail or longer tail arm generally increases the tail's ability to generate stabilizing moments.

For this preliminary design, assume:

$$
V_H=0.5
$$

This is an initial design parameter and will later be checked through stability analysis.

---

# 1.41.2 Tail Arm

The tail arm is approximately the distance between the aerodynamic reference locations of the wing and tail.

For preliminary sizing:

$$

V_H=
\frac{S_{HT}L_{HT}}
{S_{wing}MAC}

rearrange:

# 1.43 Battery Sizing

The battery is one of the most important components of an electric VTOL aircraft.

The battery must satisfy two major requirements:

1. Energy requirement
2. Power requirement

These are not the same thing.

---

## 1.43.1 Battery Energy

Battery energy is commonly expressed in:

$$
Wh
$$

where:

$$
1Wh=1W\times1hour
$$

For example, a battery that can theoretically provide 100 W for 1 hour contains approximately:

$$
100Wh
$$

of energy.

In practice, usable energy is lower because of:

* Voltage limits
* Discharge limits
* Efficiency
* Temperature
* Battery aging
* Safety reserve

---

# 1.43.2 Battery Capacity

Battery capacity is often specified in:

$$
mAh
$$

The approximate relationship between energy and capacity is:

$$
Capacity(mAh)
=
\frac{E(Wh)\times1000}
{V_{nominal}}
$$

where:

* \(E\) = energy in Wh
* \(V_{nominal}\) = nominal battery voltage

For example, if:

$$
E=300Wh
$$

and:

$$
V=22.2V
$$

then:

$$
Capacity
=
\frac{300(1000)}
{22.2}
$$

$$
Capacity\approx13514mAh
$$

or approximately:

$$
13.5Ah
$$

This is only an example.

The final battery will be selected after the mission power profile is established.

---

# 1.43.3 Why Battery Power Matters

A battery may have enough total energy but still be unable to provide the required instantaneous power.

For example:

```text
Battery A
Large energy
Low maximum current
```

may fail during VTOL take-off.

Therefore battery selection must check both:

$$
Energy
$$

and:

$$
Maximum\ Power
$$

For VTOL operation, the peak power requirement can be much higher than cruise power.

---

# 1.44 Mission Energy

The total mission energy can be approximated by integrating power over time.

For discrete flight segments:

$$
E_{mission}
=
\sum P_it_i
$$

where:

* \(P_i\) = power required during flight segment \(i\)
* \(t_i\) = duration of segment \(i\)

For example:

$$
E_{mission}
=
P_{hover}t_{hover}
+
P_{climb}t_{climb}
+
P_{cruise}t_{cruise}
+
P_{descent}t_{descent}
$$

Additional reserve should then be included.

# 1.45 Structural Sizing

* Hover loads
* Forward-flight loads
* Gust loads
* Landing loads
* Motor thrust loads
* Propeller vibration
* Maneuver loads

The first structural sizing step is to identify the major load paths.

For the wing:

```text
Lift
 ↓
Wing skin
 ↓
Spar
 ↓
Wing root
 ↓
Fuselage
```

For the VTOL system:

```text
Propeller thrust
 ↓
Motor
 ↓
Motor mount

 ↓

```

# 1.46 Wing Spar

A wing spar is one of the primary load-carrying structural members of the wing.

It resists:

* Bending
* Shear
* Torsion depending on design

For a simplified beam model, bending moment can be estimated from:

$$
M=F\,d
$$

where:

* \(M\) = bending moment
* \(F\) = applied force
* \(d\) = perpendicular distance from the force to the reference section

The actual wing bending problem is more complicated because lift is distributed along the wing.

Therefore the final spar design should use a distributed-load model rather than treating the entire wing lift as a single point load.

---

# 1.47 Load Factor

Aircraft may experience loads larger than their static weight.

A **load factor** is commonly represented by:

$$
n=\frac{L}{W}
$$

where:

* \(n\) = load factor
* \(L\) = aerodynamic lift
* \(W\) = aircraft weight

For example:

$$
n=2
$$

means the aircraft is experiencing approximately twice its weight in aerodynamic loading.

The structural design load is therefore not simply:

$$
W
$$

but may be:

$$
L=nW
$$

The appropriate design load factor must be selected according to the intended aircraft category, mission, regulations, and structural design philosophy.

For this project, detailed structural load assumptions will be established in the structural-design chapter.
